Most cost pages for Paraguay residency either quote a single "from $X" number that hides everything else, or dump a wall of unrelated fees with no structure. Here's the honest version, broken into the categories that actually drive your total: government fees, the documents you bring from home, the documents you get here, and the choice between doing it yourself and paying someone to coordinate it.

## How much does Paraguay residency cost in 2026?

There is no single number, because "residency" is at least four separate payments: the DNM government fee for your route, the background-check certificates, the apostilles and translations on your home-country documents, and the cédula once you're approved. Add them up rather than looking for one figure, and budget in guaraníes since <Fact k="fees.basis" /> — the dollar equivalent moves with both the minimum wage and the exchange rate.

These are paid directly to the relevant Paraguayan authority — DNM for the residency fee, the Policía Nacional and Identificaciones for the certificates and card. No one can "discount" a government fee; if a quote implies otherwise, ask exactly what it's discounting.

## What do the Interpol and police certificates cost?

You'll usually need two separate background checks: one from your home country (or countries you've lived in over the last three years), and one issued inside Paraguay once you've spent time here. The Paraguayan Interpol certificate runs <Fact k="fees.interpol_certificate" /> and is cash-only, so plan to have guaraníes on hand. The domestic police certificate costs <Fact k="fees.police_certificate_py" />. Your home-country certificate's price depends entirely on your own country's issuing agency — some are free, some cost more than everything else on this list combined.

One detail that catches people out regardless of country: <Fact k="documents.police_certificate_validity" />. If your document preparation runs long, an early certificate can go stale before you file, and you pay for it twice.

## What do apostilles and translations add?

Every foreign document you submit — birth certificate, marriage certificate, background check — needs an apostille from the correct authority in the country that issued it, plus a certified translation if it isn't already in Spanish. Neither of these is a Paraguayan government fee; you pay your home country's apostille office and a translator directly, and the cost varies enormously by country (some apostille services are near-free through a government portal, others require a courier and a paid intermediary). Budget this as its own line item and get a quote before you start, not after. Our [full apostille guide](/blog/apostille-for-paraguay-residency) breaks this down by document and by country type.

## How does this compare to other Latin American residency programs?

Paraguay is frequently cited as one of the more affordable residency options in the region, and the government-fee side of that reputation holds up — the fees above are modest compared to some neighboring countries' investment-residency minimums. Where the comparison gets less clean is the "extras" category: apostilles, translations and travel cost roughly the same regardless of which country you're applying to, since those costs are set by your own home country's processes, not the destination's. If you're comparing Paraguay against another country purely on a headline number, make sure both quotes are counting the same categories — government fees only, or government fees plus everything else.

## The mistake that costs people the most

It isn't any single fee — it's sequencing. People apostille a document, then discover the translation doesn't meet the standard used here, then find the underlying certificate has expired while the translation was being redone. Each fix is its own cost and its own delay. The fix is planning the order of operations before you spend a single dollar: confirm what each document needs (apostille, translation, both, in what order) before you commission any of it.
