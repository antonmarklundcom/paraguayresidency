This is one of the first questions almost every prospective resident asks, and
it deserves a direct, non-marketing answer: Paraguay is generally considered
one of the calmer countries in the region for everyday personal safety, with
the normal city-by-city and neighborhood-by-neighborhood variation you'd
expect anywhere. It is not crime-free, and it is not a place to switch off
ordinary judgment — but it also isn't the high-alert environment some
people assume based on regional stereotypes rather than the country itself.

## What "safe" actually means here

Most safety concerns for a new resident are the same ones you'd manage in any
mid-sized city worldwide: petty theft in busy public areas, standard driving
risk, and being a visibly new arrival who doesn't yet know which neighborhoods
are quiet residential streets and which are busy commercial strips at night.
Violent crime aimed specifically at foreign residents is not a defining
feature of daily life in the areas where most residents actually settle.

## City by city, briefly

- **Asunción** — the capital has calm, established residential neighborhoods
  favored by long-term expats, alongside busier central and market areas
  where normal urban caution applies.
- **Ciudad del Este** — a busy border/commercial city; the areas directly
  around the border crossing and commercial districts warrant more everyday
  awareness than the quieter residential parts of the city.
- **Encarnación and smaller interior towns** — generally quieter than either
  of the above, with a slower pace that many long-term residents specifically
  choose for that reason.

## Practical habits, not paranoia

- Get local advice on which neighborhoods suit your lifestyle before signing
  a lease — this is standard practice in any new country, not a Paraguay-
  specific red flag.
- Use the same common sense about valuables, ATMs, and late-night travel that
  you would in any unfamiliar city.
- Talk to residents who've actually lived there, not only online forums —
  perception and lived experience diverge more on this topic than almost any
  other in the relocation research process.
