The core Paraguay residency process is the same regardless of nationality, but a handful of details are specifically different for US citizens — the background check process, the flight logistics, and, most importantly, the fact that residency abroad doesn't change what the US expects from you.

## What documents does an American need that are different?

The core document list — [covered in full here](/blog/documents-you-need-for-paraguay-residency) — is the same. The specific difference is the background check: Americans typically use the FBI's national Identity History Summary rather than a state or local police certificate, because it covers your full federal record. That document then needs to be apostilled through the US Department of State's Office of Authentications, which is a distinct process from a state government's own apostille office. Budget both the FBI's processing time and the State Department's apostille turnaround into your timeline, and remember: <Fact k="documents.police_certificate_validity" />.

## How do you actually get there? (Flights)

There are no direct commercial flights from the United States to Paraguay. Realistic routing typically connects through a regional hub — commonly Panama City, São Paulo, or Buenos Aires — before a final leg into Asunción. Build this into your trip planning, especially if you're coordinating a filing appointment against a specific travel window; a missed or delayed connection has more consequences when your whole trip is built around one appointment.

## Does US tax change because you live in Paraguay?

No, and this is the single most important thing for an American reader of this page to understand clearly: the United States taxes its citizens on worldwide income based on citizenship, not residence. Living in Paraguay, holding Paraguayan residency, or even eventually becoming a Paraguayan tax resident does not end your US filing obligation. Paraguay's own system is territorial — <Fact k="tax.foreign_income_treatment" /> — which is a real and useful feature of Paraguay's tax code, but it says nothing about what the US still expects from you as a citizen. Mechanisms exist (foreign earned income exclusion, foreign tax credits) that can reduce a US tax bill in some situations, but whether and how much they apply to you depends on your specific income and circumstances. **Confirm your position with a US tax adviser** — this page cannot and does not give you a number.

## What about banking?

Once you hold a cédula, opening a Paraguayan bank account is realistic — see [the full banking guide](/blog/opening-a-bank-account-in-paraguay) for the general process. US citizens specifically go through FATCA-related compliance steps on the bank's side, since Paraguayan banks holding US-citizen accounts have reporting obligations to US authorities. This doesn't prevent Americans from banking here, but it can add friction or extra documentation at some institutions more than others — ask directly when comparing banks.

## What about state-issued documents like a birth certificate?

Unlike the FBI check, a US birth or marriage certificate is issued at the state (or sometimes county) level, and each state has its own apostille authority — usually the Secretary of State's office. This is a different process from the federal apostille your FBI check needs, so don't assume one covers the other; you may be dealing with two separate apostille authorities (a state office and the US Department of State) for two different documents in the same application, each with its own processing time.

## Do Americans need a visa to enter Paraguay before applying for residency?

US citizens can generally enter Paraguay as tourists without a visa for an initial stay, which is enough time to begin the in-person portions of the residency process for many applicants. Confirm the current entry requirement before you travel, since visa-free arrangements and their permitted duration can change, and plan your document preparation (especially the FBI check and its apostille) to be substantially complete before you fly, so your time in-country is spent on filing rather than waiting on paperwork from home.

## The mistake that costs people the most

Assuming a state-level background check will be accepted, or assuming that living abroad quietly ends a US filing obligation. Both assumptions surface late — usually when a document is rejected during filing, or years later when unfiled US returns become a much larger problem than they would have been handled from the start. Get the FBI/State Department process right the first time, and get an honest answer from a US tax professional early rather than after the fact.

## What about Americans with a criminal record or prior visa issues?

A prior criminal record doesn't automatically disqualify an applicant, but it does need to be disclosed honestly and documented accurately through the background-check process rather than hidden — an application built on an incomplete background check creates a far bigger problem later than an honest disclosure does upfront. If you have a specific concern here, it's worth raising directly and early with whoever is helping you file, rather than discovering how it's handled partway through the process.
