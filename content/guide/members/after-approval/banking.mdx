Banking is where expectations and reality diverge most for new residents. Online you will read that anyone can open a Paraguayan account with a passport in an afternoon. Sometimes a branch will do that. More often, in 2026, a bank wants a cédula, proof of address, proof of income and a clear account of where your money comes from — and it can say no without explaining why.

This chapter tells you what to expect, how to prepare a file that gets approved, and how to live comfortably while you wait.

## The honest picture

Paraguayan banks operate under anti-money-laundering rules supervised by SEPRELAD and the central bank. Those rules put the burden on the bank to understand who you are and where your money comes from. For a newly arrived foreigner with income from abroad, that means paperwork. Policies differ between banks, and sometimes between branches of the same bank, and they change without notice. Nobody can promise you an account.

What we can tell you is what makes a file easy to approve:

- a **cédula** (chapter 6), not only a passport
- a **Paraguayan address** backed by a lease or utility bill
- **documented income** that matches the money you plan to move in
- a **reason for the account** that fits the rest of the file
- for some banks, a **RUC**

If you already have the cédula, a RUC and the income evidence you are preparing for permanent residency under Res. 407/2026 (chapter 2), you have most of what a bank wants. That overlap is useful: build one evidence file and use it twice.

## What banks usually ask for

| Item | Why they ask | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Cédula | Identity in the Paraguayan system | Some banks open with a passport and resident card; many wait for the cédula. |
| Passport | Identity and nationality | Bring the original. |
| Proof of address | Where you live | Lease, or a utility bill in your name (ANDE electricity, ESSAP water). |
| Proof of income | How the account will be funded | Employment contract, payslips, pension statements, client invoices, recent bank statements. |
| Source of funds for large transfers | AML rules | Sale deeds, inheritance documents, investment statements. |
| RUC | Tax registration | Required by some banks, especially for business activity. |
| Tax self-certification | International reporting (CRS; FATCA for US persons) | You declare your tax residences and tax identification numbers. |
| Reference letter | Relationship history | Some banks ask for a letter from your current bank. |

Foreign documents used as proof of income may need to be apostilled and translated, like your residency documents. Ask before you order.

## US persons

If you are a US citizen or US tax resident, say so up front. Under FATCA, the bank must identify US persons and report their accounts to the US through Paraguay's arrangements. Some banks accept US clients readily; others decline them because the compliance cost isn't worth it to them. You will complete a US tax form (a W-9) as part of opening. None of this is a reason for panic; it is a reason to ask the bank directly whether it accepts US persons before you spend a morning in its branch.

## Guaraníes, dollars, or both?

Most banks offer accounts in guaraníes and in US dollars. Many residents keep both: guaraníes for daily life, dollars for savings or for receiving foreign income. Rent and many larger purchases, including property, are often priced in dollars; supermarkets and utilities are in guaraníes.

## Step by step: opening your first account

1. **Shortlist two or three banks.** Ask expats and your accountant which ones currently accept foreign residents with your profile — US person, business owner, retiree, remote worker.
2. **Ask before you go.** Phone or message the branch: which documents, is an appointment needed, do they accept your nationality, is a RUC required.
3. **Build the file.** Cédula, passport, proof of address, income evidence covering several recent months, a short written explanation of your income and how you will use the account.
4. **Match amounts.** If you say your income is a certain monthly amount, the statements must show it. Inconsistencies trigger questions.
5. **Attend the appointment.** Expect forms in Spanish, your signature many times, and questions about your work and your plans. Answer consistently with your residency file.
6. **Wait for compliance review.** The branch forwards your file. Approval can take days or weeks.
7. **Test with a small transfer** before moving anything large. Confirm the incoming-transfer details (SWIFT code, intermediary bank for dollars, beneficiary name exactly as the bank records it).

## Moving money in

Large incoming transfers draw questions. Before you send a significant sum:

- **Tell the bank in advance** and ask what documents it wants for that transfer.
- **Keep the paper trail**: the sale contract, the investment statement, the inheritance document. Apostille and translation may be needed for foreign documents.
- **Send from an account in your own name** where possible. Third-party transfers are slower to clear.
- **Know your home country's side.** Some countries have reporting requirements on large outgoing transfers or foreign accounts. Confirm with your own adviser.

For the Investor Pass (chapter 10), the source-of-funds file is a formal part of the process, not a bank preference. Build it before you invest.

## While you wait: living without a local account

You don't need a Paraguayan account on day one. Most new arrivals manage like this:

- **International cards.** Visa and Mastercard are widely accepted in Asunción's supermarkets, restaurants and larger shops. Carry cash for smaller places and markets.
- **ATMs.** Withdraw guaraníes with a foreign card. Fees vary a lot between machines and banks; find one that suits you and stick to it.
- **Multi-currency accounts.** Services like Wise are useful for receiving and converting money, though what they support for guaraníes changes; check before relying on them.
- **Cash for official fees.** Several government offices take cash only (the Interpol certificate, for example). Keep guaraníes on hand.

## Why accounts get refused (and what to do)

- **No cédula yet.** Wait, or try a bank that accepts the resident card.
- **Income evidence doesn't match the planned deposits.** Fix the file; explain the gap in writing.
- **Unclear source of funds.** Provide documents, not explanations.
- **Nationality risk appetite.** Some banks avoid certain nationalities or US persons. Move on to another bank.
- **Inconsistent answers.** If your residency file says retiree and your bank interview says consultant, you have a problem. Keep one story, because there is only one true one.

A refusal at one bank does not mean a refusal everywhere. Don't argue with a branch; go to the next one with a better file.

## Business accounts

A company account needs the company's documents, its RUC, the officers' cédulas, and usually more source-of-funds evidence. If you plan the productive Investor Pass route (chapter 10), your business bank account is part of how you prove the investment was made. Start that conversation early with your accountant and the bank.

## Keeping the account healthy

- Update the bank when your residency changes from temporary to permanent and when you renew your cédula.
- Respond to periodic information requests; banks refresh client files and can freeze accounts that don't reply.
- Keep your tax self-certification current. If your tax residency changes, tell the bank.

## Questions to ask a bank before you visit

- Do you open accounts for foreign residents who have a resident card but no cédula yet?
- Do you accept clients of my nationality? US persons?
- Which documents do you need for proof of income from abroad? Do they need an apostille?
- Is a RUC required?
- Can I open accounts in both guaraníes and dollars at the same appointment?
- What are the fees for incoming international transfers in dollars, and which intermediary bank do you use?
- Can I use online banking and a mobile app in English, or only Spanish?

Write the answers down with the name of the person who gave them and the date. If the branch later asks for something different, you can point to what you were told.

## Paying for things in daily life

Once you have a local account, daily payments get easier: debit cards on the local networks, instant transfers between Paraguayan banks, and QR payments in many shops and restaurants. Bills for electricity, water and phone can usually be paid through your bank app or at payment points. Landlords commonly want rent by bank transfer, and receiving your deposit back is simpler into a local account.

## Your next step

Start a banking folder now with scans of everything in the table above. When your cédula arrives, you will already have the rest. If your income comes from several sources, write a one-page summary of each — source, amount, frequency, supporting document — because that page answers most of a compliance officer's questions before they ask.
