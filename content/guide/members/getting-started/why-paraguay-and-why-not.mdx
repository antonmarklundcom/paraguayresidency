We are a small team in Asunción and we put people through Paraguayan immigration every week. The single most useful thing we can tell you before you order a birth certificate is this: Paraguay residency is a good tool for a specific kind of person, and a poor one for several others. The advertisements don't make that distinction. This chapter does.

Read it with a pen. At the end you will write down three things: your reason to go ahead, your strongest reason to pause, and the one piece of missing information that would change your mind. If you can't fill in the first line with something better than "the internet said it was easy", stop there and save yourself the fees.

## What Paraguay actually offers

Strip away the marketing and the proposition is four things.

**A residency that is realistic to get.** Paraguay's immigration law (Ley 6984/2022) gives foreigners a temporary residency of <Fact k="temporary.duration" />. There is no investment or deposit on the standard route — the old fixed bank deposit is <Fact k="solvency.deposit_abolished" />. What you need is a clean record, a correctly prepared set of documents, and a few days in Asunción.

**A tax system built around where income is earned.** Paraguay taxes Paraguay-source income; <Fact k="tax.foreign_income_treatment" />. Personal income from Paraguayan sources is taxed at <Fact k="tax.territorial_rate" />. That is the Paraguayan side only. Your home country may still have a claim on you, and chapter 8 exists to stop you assuming otherwise.

**A cost of living that is low by international standards.** Crowd-sourced data puts everyday spending at <Fact k="costofliving.overview" />, and <Fact k="costofliving.monthly_family" />. Rent comes on top; chapter 4 shows how we budget it.

**A path to citizenship for those who stay.** Naturalisation requires <Fact k="citizenship.years" />, plus a judicial process, lawful occupation and good conduct. It is a real path, but it is a path for people who actually live here — not a passport-by-post.

## What changed in 2026 — read this even if you researched last year

Most of what you will find online was written before 2026. Four changes matter.

1. **Presence is now actively enforced.** DNM Resolution 376/2026 has the migration authority sweep the register twice a year and cancel residencies of people who have been away too long. For temporary residents the rule is <Fact k="temporary.presence_rule" />. For permanent residents it is <Fact k="permanent.presence_rule" />. The rules themselves come from the 2022 law; what is new is that someone now checks.
2. **Permanent residency needs proof of means.** Under DNM Resolution 407/2026, converting to permanent now requires <Fact k="solvency.requirement" />. A university degree on its own is no longer enough. Chapter 2 walks through the categories.
3. **Government fees went up.** DNM fees are <Fact k="fees.basis" />. Any page quoting the older guaraní amounts is out of date.
4. **There is a new Investor Pass.** <Fact k="investorpass.legal_instrument" />. It skips the temporary stage for people making a qualifying investment. Chapter 10 covers it.

None of these makes Paraguay a worse choice for someone who intends to live here. They make it a worse choice for someone who wanted a card in a drawer without ever coming back.

## It suits you if…

| You are… | Why Paraguay tends to work |
|---|---|
| A remote worker or freelancer with foreign clients | Income earned abroad is generally outside Paraguayan income tax, and the residency does not depend on a local job. |
| A retiree with a pension or savings | Low running costs, a straightforward route, and no investment requirement on the standard path. |
| A family wanting a lower-cost base in South America | Dependants can apply alongside you (chapter 9), and daily costs for a household are modest. |
| An entrepreneur willing to build something local | Company formation and the productive Investor Pass route reward people who create jobs here. |
| Someone who wants a genuine second home and will visit at least once a year | The presence rules are easy to meet if you really come back. |

## Look elsewhere if…

| You are… | Why we would hesitate |
|---|---|
| Hoping residency alone will end your home-country tax obligations | It won't. US citizens in particular remain taxable at home whatever their residence. Confirm your position with your own adviser before you move anything. |
| Planning never to return after the first visit | Enforcement under Res. 376/2026 means a card you never use is a card you will lose. |
| Dependent on specialist medical care | Asunción has good private hospitals, but specialist coverage thins out fast outside the capital. Check your specific specialty before deciding. |
| Unwilling to deal with Spanish-language bureaucracy | Every office works in Spanish. You can hire help, but you cannot outsource your patience. |
| Relying on a deadline you cannot move | Nobody controls the processing window. A job start date or a lease end that cannot slip is a poor foundation. |

## The honest downsides

We live here and we like it. Here is what we would tell a friend.

**The heat is serious.** Summers in Asunción are long and very hot. Air conditioning is not a luxury in a flat; check that the one you rent actually works before you sign.

**Bureaucracy is slow and in person.** Many steps require you to attend, often more than once, and a single mismatched name between two documents can send you back to the start of a queue. Chapter 11 lists the mistakes we see monthly for exactly this reason.

**The language gap is real outside the expat bubble.** Spanish is essential; Guaraní is widely spoken, especially outside Asunción. English will get you through a hotel check-in and not much else.

**Infrastructure is uneven.** Fibre internet is good in Asunción, Encarnación and Ciudad del Este, and patchy in the countryside. Power cuts happen in storms. Roads outside the main routes can be rough.

**Residency is not a travel document.** Your Paraguayan residency does not change what your passport lets you do. It does not give you visa-free access anywhere you didn't already have it.

## Separate the card from the life

People mix up four different outcomes. Write each one down separately.

- **Immigration status.** A lawful right to live in Paraguay, which you keep by meeting the presence rules.
- **Tax position.** Where you are tax resident and where your income is taxed. This is decided by facts and by each country's rules, not by the card. Paraguay issues a tax residency certificate — chapter 6 covers when and how — and that certificate has its own evidence requirements.
- **Banking.** Whether a Paraguayan bank will open an account for you. Chapter 7 is candid about this.
- **Daily life.** Housing, schooling, health care, work routines, friends.

Residency delivers the first one. It makes the other three possible, but it doesn't deliver them. If your plan only works when all four fall into place at once, identify which one is most likely to fail and test it first.

## Make the scouting visit do real work

If you haven't been to Paraguay, go before you file — or combine the two, since you have to come to Asunción to file anyway (chapter 5). Treat the trip as research, not a holiday.

1. **Work your normal hours from where you would live.** Run your actual calls and uploads, not a speed test the landlord screenshotted.
2. **Walk the daily routes.** Supermarket, pharmacy, gym, school run. Asunción is spread out and many people rely on ride-hailing apps; see what that means for your day.
3. **Visit two neighbourhoods at different times.** A quiet street at 10am can be a different place at 7pm on a Friday.
4. **Ask about the flat's details.** Air conditioning, water pressure, generator or not, who fixes things and how fast.
5. **Check the non-negotiables.** If a child needs a particular school or someone needs a specific doctor, visit and ask your questions in person.
6. **Keep three lists.** What you saw, what someone promised, and what still needs checking. Promises go on the second list until they move to the first.

## Five questions to answer before chapter 2

1. What will you actually do differently once you have residency?
2. How often, realistically, will you be in Paraguay in each of the next three years?
3. Where does your income come from, and does it keep flowing if your address changes?
4. Who else is moving with you, and does each of them have a workable daily life here?
5. What is your fallback if the process takes longer than you hoped?

If question 2 produces "almost never", re-read the presence rules above before going any further. If question 3 has an uncertain answer, fix that before you fix your documents.

## Where people actually settle

"Paraguay" is not one experience. Where you live changes almost everything above.

- **Asunción and its suburbs** (Villa Morra, Carmelitas, Recoleta, Lambaré, Luque, San Lorenzo): where the offices, hospitals, international schools, airport and most of the work are. Every residency applicant spends time here, and most foreigners live here or within commuting distance.
- **San Bernardino and Lake Ypacaraí**: a lake town an hour or so from the capital, busy in summer, quiet the rest of the year. Popular with people who want space but still need Asunción regularly.
- **Encarnación**: the southern city on the Paraná river, across the bridge from Posadas in Argentina, with a newer riverfront and a growing number of foreign residents.
- **Ciudad del Este**: the commercial border city with Brazil. Busy, trade-driven, not for everyone.
- **The Chaco and the colonies**: agricultural communities in the west and centre of the country, some with long-established immigrant populations. Remote, hot, and very different from the capital.

Choose the place before you fall in love with the process. A residency card is the same everywhere; your daily life is not.
