Your nationality matters, but a passport label is not a complete document checklist. Where you were born, where records were issued, where you have lived and the route you are using can all affect the work. We want you to finish this chapter with a document map that someone can review, rather than a folder of expensive papers ordered on assumptions.

Start with the route agreed in chapter 2, The routes compared. Ask for the current list for that route and your circumstances before requesting replacement certificates. A document can be authentic and still be the wrong document for the application.

## Build a record for each document

Prepare an inventory covering your passport, civil records, background-check documents and any evidence specific to your household or route. Treat this as a review list, not a claim that every item is required in every case.

For each item, record the issuing country, issuing authority, exact document type, name shown, language, issue date, original location and proposed authentication path. Add the person responsible for the next action. "Birth certificate ordered" is less useful than knowing which version was ordered, who issues it and where it will go next.

Explain changes of name, different spellings and missing records before authentication. The reviewer needs to see how the person on a birth record connects to the person on the passport. An apostille does not resolve a mismatch in identity details.

## Separate nationality from place of issue

A British passport holder born elsewhere may need civil records from that other country. An American applicant should establish which authority issued a particular record before asking how to authenticate it. A German applicant should check the requested record format and language. A South African applicant should establish the issuing and authentication sequence before paying a courier.

These are questions to resolve, not separate universal checklists for those nationalities. The useful distinction is between your immigration eligibility and each document's journey. Citizenship may affect the former; the issuing jurisdiction and record type help determine the latter.

List your residence history accurately and ask which background checks it triggers. Do not substitute a remembered look-back period from a forum for the current requirements. Tell the reviewer if a country makes certificates difficult to obtain from abroad so that the issue is addressed before travel.

## Authentication: confirm the chain before sending originals

An apostille authenticates the origin of a public document under the applicable convention framework. Use the [HCCH Apostille Section](https://www.hcch.net/Apostille) to identify the relevant authorities and check applicability. Citizenship alone does not tell you which authority can authenticate a record.

Ask whether the particular document can go directly to that authority or needs an earlier certification step. Also check whether the receiving office wants an original, an accepted certified copy or another format. Paying for authentication of the wrong version leaves you with an authenticated mistake.

Where an apostille route is unavailable, obtain the legalisation sequence from the relevant authorities. Write down the handoffs, delivery arrangements and return address. Keep scans before dispatch and use tracking. Avoid sending an irreplaceable original until you understand whether it is actually required.

## Translation: approve the method first

Do not assume that every translation made abroad is unacceptable, or that any certified translation will do. The [Migraciones temporary-residence requirements](https://migraciones.gov.py/residencia-temporal/) describe translation options, including conditions for translations prepared abroad. Confirm the rule for your actual route before commissioning work.

Send the translator the full document package the reviewer says must be translated, including authentication material where applicable. Ask how names, stamps and annotations will be handled. Check the completed translation against your passport and the original before it joins the filing set.

If you already have a translation, send a scan for review before paying to replace it. If it is not acceptable, ask precisely why: translator credentials, authentication, missing pages or a content error call for different remedies.

## Coordinate freshness with delivery

Ask which records have an acceptance window and what event that window is measured against. Record the confirmed answer beside the document. Do not assume that every civil record needs to be newly issued, or that authentication resets the age of a police certificate.

Work backwards from a realistic filing plan. Investigate slow issuing processes early, but coordinate ordering time-sensitive documents with the rest of the chain. "Order everything immediately" and "leave the police certificate until last" are both poor universal rules.

Chapter 5, Timeline, week by week, turns those dependencies into a schedule. Your aim here is to identify which document controls the schedule and which can be prepared without starting a validity clock.

## Review the package before travel

Ask for a scan review while corrections are still easier to make. Include both sides where relevant and every attachment; a cropped photo can hide the issue that matters. Record which version was reviewed and what remains outstanding.

Keep originals organised separately from working copies. Use a secure channel for identity documents and avoid circulating complete files in group chats. For a household, chapter 9, Family, explains how to keep shared evidence connected to each person's file.

**Ready means:** the required document has been identified, its format and authentication checked, its timing assessed and its translation plan agreed. Marking those steps in chapter 12, Checklists, is far more useful than simply ticking "documents collected".
