The number you need is the cost of reaching a usable outcome, including the work that happens outside the application itself. A cheap filing quote can be perfectly accurate and still leave you unprepared for documents, travel and the steps after approval. We separate those costs so you can see what you are committing to.

This chapter gives you a budgeting method, not invented ranges. Where an amount has not been confirmed for your case, leave the amount open and identify who will supply it. An honest blank is more useful than a precise-looking estimate borrowed from someone else's application.

## Define what the quote delivers

Start by writing the finish line beside each quote: document review, submission, response to queries, approval support, identity-document assistance or a later residency application. Ask whether the quote covers the applicant alone or a household, and whether tax and banking support are included or separately arranged.

For temporary-residency service pricing, our reference is <Fact k="pricing.temporary" />. For a permanent-residency service, it is <Fact k="pricing.permanent" />. Neither should be mistaken for the whole relocation budget.

Request the scope and exclusions in writing. Ask who pays when an additional certificate is required, a translation needs correction or an appointment changes. Distinguish correction of a provider's mistake from genuinely new work caused by changed circumstances. The point is to know the rule before a disagreement arises.

## Give every expense a category and a payee

Build a budget with separate sections for official charges, document preparation, professional services, travel and post-approval setup. Against every line, record the payee, currency, payment trigger, evidence of the amount and whether it is included elsewhere.

Do not assume every administrative step attracts a government fee. A provider may charge for assistance with a process independently of any official charge. Ask for those lines separately and for an official receipt where an official payment is involved.

Mark estimates as estimates and keep the date of each quote. If a charge is supplied in a different currency from the money you hold, ask your payment provider about conversion and transfer costs. Your budget should reflect the amount leaving your account, not only the amount the recipient expects.

## Price the document journey

Chapter 3, Documents, apostilles and translations, by nationality, identifies the documents. Here, follow each one from issuer to filing. Possible cost items include replacement records, authentication, translation, certification of copies, delivery and correction of defects.

A household may share some evidence while still needing separate filing copies or translations. Ask the reviewer which work can actually be shared before multiplying every line by the number of applicants. Equally, do not assume a family package includes every dependent's unusual records.

Obtain a translation quote from the complete reviewed set. Missing attachments can change the job after the first quote. If documents cross borders, include the return journey and consider what you would do if an original were delayed. A courier charge is only part of the exposure when replacement would disrupt filing.

## Budget for travel decisions, not a promised trip count

Your travel plan depends on personal-attendance requirements and the sequence confirmed for your case. Chapter 5, Timeline, week by week, covers those dependencies. Ask which steps require you, which can be handled remotely and which need a later appointment.

Compare a longer stay with returning later, using actual accommodation and travel quotes. Include local transport, appointment-day flexibility, childcare and work you cannot do while attending offices. Cheap accommodation far from the places you must visit can add practical cost.

Keep a separate contingency line for a plausible disruption: rescheduled attendance, a document correction or an extra courier. Build it from what that disruption would require. An arbitrary cushion is less informative than knowing which problem your reserve could cover.

## Include the steps after approval

Cédula assistance has its own scope: <Fact k="pricing.cedula" />. Tax and RUC assistance is also separate: <Fact k="pricing.tax_residency" />. Ask what each service delivers and whether ongoing support is a different engagement.

Chapter 6, Cédula and RUC, explains the handover. Chapter 7, Banking, covers practical account preparation. Account terms, housing deposits and local setup belong in your cash plan even when they are not immigration expenses.

Separate money spent from money temporarily committed. A refundable deposit is still unavailable while held, and an investment is not equivalent to a filing fee. If you are considering the Investor Pass, chapter 10, Investor Pass overview, helps you assess the capital decision separately.

## Compare quotes without losing the detail

Put competing proposals against the same scope. For each missing line, write "excluded", "included" or "not answered". Do not count silence as inclusion. Ask who handles authority queries and how you will receive evidence that work has been completed.

Finally, create a payment calendar based on milestones and agreed terms. Check whether several costs fall due before the result you are waiting for. A total you can afford may still create a cash-flow problem if the timing is wrong.

**Your output:** a budget whose major lines have a payee, a scope and a payment trigger, plus a list of unresolved amounts. You should be able to explain what an adverse delay would cost without pretending to know when approval will arrive.
