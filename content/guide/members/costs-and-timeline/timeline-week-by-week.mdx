A useful weekly plan tells you what to do next and what evidence allows you to move forward. It does not assign an approval date to a government queue. We organise the process around readiness, filing, review and the work after approval, then use weekly check-ins to keep those stages moving.

The processing reference is <Fact k="residency.timeline" />. Treat any personal schedule as a working plan built around that uncertainty. If an adviser offers a finish date, ask which parts they control and what assumptions support it.

## Before you assign calendar dates

Agree the route from chapter 2, The routes compared, and the document list from chapter 3, Documents, apostilles and translations, by nationality. Record the personal-attendance steps and ask what can be prepared remotely. Without those answers, a travel calendar is guesswork.

Make a task list with an owner, a dependency and evidence of completion for each item. "Application ready" is not a useful task. "Reviewer has checked the complete scans and identified no outstanding document corrections" is something you can verify.

Identify the chain that determines the earliest possible filing. It may involve an overseas record, authentication and translation. Other work can proceed alongside that chain, but finishing the easy items does not shorten the slowest dependency.

## Your first planning week: investigate the bottlenecks

Use the initial planning session to contact issuers, confirm document formats and understand delivery arrangements. Ask which records have acceptance windows and how those windows relate to filing. Investigating an issuing process does not require ordering every time-sensitive certificate immediately.

Resolve name discrepancies and missing civil records early. A correction that needs another authority is more disruptive than making an extra copy. For a household, check the slowest person's file as well as the lead applicant's; chapter 9, Family, explains why their readiness may differ.

End the week with a short status note: confirmed, waiting on someone else, and blocked. A blocked item needs a decision or action, not another hopeful completion date.

## The preparation weeks: review before committing

As documents arrive, compare them with the agreed specification. Send complete scans for review and keep track of which version was accepted for the next step. Do not assume that receipt from the issuer means readiness for submission.

Coordinate authentication and translation in the confirmed order. If something is rejected during review, ask what exactly must change and whether the correction affects another document's timing. Update the filing plan rather than quietly retaining the old travel assumptions.

Book around confirmed attendance arrangements and a realistic readiness assessment. When you must book earlier, understand the cancellation and change terms and the consequence of documents arriving late. Chapter 4, Costs, real ones, belongs beside the calendar because schedule changes have costs.

## The arrival and filing week: capture evidence

Prepare a practical appointment pack: the reviewed originals, requested copies, appointment information and contact details. Confirm where you are going and who is accompanying you. Leave room to resolve a local administrative issue instead of filling the entire visit with unrelated commitments.

After submission, retain the receipt or acknowledgement, reference information and a copy of what was filed. Ask who will receive requests for further information and how those requests will reach you. Check that the contact details in the file are usable while you travel.

Do not infer permission to stay, work or re-enter solely from having attended an appointment. Ask the adviser to explain your actual status and the document supporting it before making travel or employment decisions. The answer must match the route and stage of your application.

## The waiting weeks: distinguish silence from a problem

Government review is a separate dependency even when your file is complete. Avoid the assumption that every delay proves someone has made a mistake. Equally, do not accept "processing" indefinitely without knowing what was submitted and how follow-up is handled.

Agree a communication rhythm with your representative. A useful update identifies the latest confirmed action, any outstanding request, who owns the response and the next follow-up trigger. It need not contain invented progress to be valuable.

If a query arrives, establish the response needed before ordering replacements. Keep the request and your response together. Ask whether it changes the timing or requires personal attendance. When a fixed commitment approaches, review the fallback plan rather than pressuring someone to promise approval.

## The approval handover: start the next calendar

Approval should trigger a document and next-step review. Check personal details, retain the decision and ask what must happen before you can apply for or collect the identity document. Cédula timing: <Fact k="cedula.timeline" />.

Tax and RUC work has its own timing: <Fact k="tax.timeline" />. Chapter 6, Cédula and RUC, covers the sequence and the handover questions. Do not schedule every post-approval task on the assumption that the first approval completes them all.

**Your weekly routine:** review the blocking item, confirm the next action and revise commitments affected by new information. Use chapter 12, Checklists, to record completion. This makes the calendar useful even when the final date remains uncertain.
