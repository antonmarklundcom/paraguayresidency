If you are weighing a second residency in South or Central America, Paraguay,
Uruguay, and Panama probably all showed up on your list. That is not a
coincidence. All three sit outside the immigration systems most Northern
investors are used to, all three have a reputation for being reachable
without a residency-by-donation price tag, and all three give you a foothold
in Latin America without requiring you to move there full time. Beyond that,
the similarities thin out fast.

This is not a "which one wins" article. It is a walk through what actually
differs, so you can match the program to your situation instead of picking
the one with the best marketing.

## Why these three end up compared

Uruguay and Panama have been on investor-residency shortlists for decades.
Paraguay is newer to that conversation, at least newer to the part of it
that reaches people outside the Southern Cone. What the three have in common
is geography and posture: none of them requires you to renounce anything,
none of them taxes worldwide income the way your home country might, and
none of them expects you to live there year-round to keep your status. From
that shared starting point, they diverge on speed, cost profile, and how
established the expat infrastructure already is.

If you are also trying to place these options against others outside the
region, the [route finder](/route-finder) is built for that side-by-side

## Paraguay's actual differentiator

Paraguay's standard immigration path, like most countries, runs you through
temporary residency before you are eligible for permanent status. The
Investor Pass, introduced <Fact k="investorpass.launch_date" />, lets a
qualifying investor skip that first stage and apply for permanent residency
directly. That is the whole mechanism. It is not a faster processing lane
for the same application; it removes a stage that other applicants still
have to serve.

The investment starts <Fact k="investorpass.min_investment_usd" />, and the
card issued is valid for <Fact k="investorpass.validity_years" />. We keep
repeating that these figures should be confirmed against the current
resolution before you commit funds, because published numbers on this
program have not always agreed with each other, and we would rather send
you to the source than let a stale figure sit on a page.

The other piece of Paraguay's appeal is its tax system. Paraguay taxes on a
<Fact k="tax.territorial_rate" /> basis, and <Fact k="tax.foreign_income_treatment" />.
Pair that with <Fact k="permanent.presence_rule" /> and you have a
residency that does not force you to restructure your life around meeting a
presence test just to keep your card active. For more on how the investment
itself can be structured, see [investment routes](/investor-pass/investment-routes).

None of that makes Paraguay automatically the right call. It makes it a
specific kind of tool: fast entry to permanent status, a territorial tax
system, and a lighter presence obligation, aimed at someone who wants a
foothold without a long on-ramp.

## Where Uruguay's appeal comes from

Uruguay is the most established of the three with retirees, remote workers,
and people who want a stable base with a strong rule-of-law reputation.
Its residency options lean more on tax-residency criteria than on a single
investment-and-done step, and the country has decades of practice handling
foreigners moving through that system. If you value predictability that
comes from a long track record, and you are comfortable with a process that
may ask more of your time and physical presence along the way, Uruguay is
worth serious attention.

We are not going to state Uruguay's current investment thresholds, fee
schedules, or timelines here. Those figures move, they are not something
this site verifies, and stating them with false confidence would do you a
disservice. If Uruguay is genuinely in your consideration set, talk to an
immigration specialist licensed there and get the current rules in writing
before you compare them against anything you read on a Paraguay-focused
site, including this one.

## Where Panama's appeal comes from

Panama's Friendly Nations Visa and its other investor and pensioner
programs are well-trodden ground. There is a large, mature expat community
already built around them, English is widely spoken in the areas where
expats concentrate, and the country's dollarized economy removes a currency
question that comes up in most of its neighbors. For someone who wants an
established community to land in rather than a newer program with less
history behind it, Panama has a real advantage.

The same caveat applies here as with Uruguay. Panama's specific investment
minimums, program categories, and processing timelines are not figures we
track or verify, and they change. If Panama is on your shortlist, get
current numbers from a Panamanian immigration attorney rather than treating
any secondhand summary, including ours, as settled fact.

## The actual trade-off

Line the three up honestly and the pattern looks like this. Paraguay offers
a shorter route to a permanent card for a qualifying investor and a
territorial tax system with a manageable presence rule. Uruguay offers a
longer track record, a tax-residency-based framework, and a reputation many
investors find reassuring precisely because it is not new. Panama offers
the most built-out expat infrastructure of the three and a dollarized,
English-friendly environment that lowers the day-to-day friction of
actually living there.

None of that ranks them. A retiree who wants an established community and
does not mind a more involved qualifying process may be better served by
Panama. Someone prioritizing a long-term, high-confidence tax domicile with
decades of precedent behind it may prefer Uruguay. Someone who wants
permanent status quickly, values a territorial tax system, and does not
need a large existing expat scene to feel comfortable is the person the
Investor Pass was built for.

The honest answer to "which is best" is that it is not one question. It is
three separate questions about what you value most, and the three countries
answer them differently. Work out which profile actually describes you
first, then let the program follow from that.
