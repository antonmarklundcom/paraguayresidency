Real estate is the route most applicants ask about first. It feels concrete
next to bonds or business capital: you can see the property, you can walk
through it, you can point to it. That familiarity is also why it causes the
most avoidable mistakes. This piece is about what actually gets filed, not
about which neighborhood to buy in.

## What counts as qualifying real estate

The route sets a minimum committed to Paraguayan real property at
<Fact k="investorpass.route_real_estate_usd" />. That figure sits inside the
broader <Fact k="investorpass.min_investment_usd" /> threshold the program
uses across its routes, so the real estate purchase has to clear the bar on
its own, not as part of a mixed basket unless your case is structured that
way from the start.

Qualifying means registered title in your name (or in an entity structure we
have already reviewed), a purchase price that holds up against an
independent valuation, and paperwork that a residency officer can check
against the public registry without asking you for anything you can't
produce quickly. It does not mean any house, any lot, or any unit in any
building. See [investment routes](/investor-pass/investment-routes#real_estate)
for how this route sits next to the others before you commit funds to one.

## Why documentation for this route is comparatively easy

Compared to a business-capital route, where you're proving ongoing
operations, payroll, and cash flow, real estate is a snapshot. The deed
exists or it doesn't. The registry entry exists or it doesn't. A licensed
appraiser can confirm value in one visit. There's no ledger to reconstruct
and no argument about what counts as "invested" versus "spent."

That's the appeal, and it's real. A clean purchase with a clean deed is one
of the more straightforward files we put together. But "comparatively easy"
is not the same as "no work." The registry entry has to be current, the
chain of title has to be unbroken, and the valuation has to be dated close
enough to the filing that nobody can argue the number has moved. We check
all three before we tell a client the property is ready to use.

## Title and valuation documentation, in practice

For the filing we need the registered deed (escritura), a certificate of no
liens from the property registry, and a valuation that an independent party
signed, not the seller's asking price and not a number pulled from a
listing site. If the property changed hands more than once in recent years,
we trace that chain back far enough to be sure nothing in it is contested.

None of this is exotic. A Paraguayan notary and the property registry
produce most of it as a matter of course. Where it slows down is when a
seller is disorganized, when a prior sale was never properly recorded, or
when the property sits in an area where surveys are older than the current
parcel boundaries. Those are fixable, but they take weeks, not days, and
they should be sorted before you wire money, not after.

## Practical traps

**Off-plan purchases.** Buying a unit that doesn't exist yet, on the promise
of future title, is common in Paraguay's growing developments. It's a
reasonable investment decision on its own terms. It is usually not a
residency filing on its own terms, because there is no registered title to
show yet. If you want to use a real estate purchase for the Pass, plan
around when title actually transfers, not around the sales brochure's
timeline.

**Unclear or partial title.** Rural land in particular sometimes carries
title that is real but incomplete — shared ownership among heirs, an old
survey that doesn't match current boundaries, or a sale that was agreed but
never formally registered. Any of these can be resolved, but resolving them
takes time you may not have if you're trying to file on a schedule.

**Overvaluation to hit the threshold.** We've seen buyers ask a seller or an
accommodating appraiser to inflate a sale price so a property that's really
worth less clears the minimum on paper. Don't do this. An independent
valuation that doesn't match the market invites exactly the scrutiny you're
trying to avoid, and it puts the whole application at risk over a gap that
was avoidable from the start. If a property is short of the threshold,
either add a second property, add cash into another eligible instrument, or
choose a different route.

**Financing structures.** A mortgage or seller-financed purchase changes
what counts as your own committed capital versus borrowed money. Get this
reviewed before you sign anything, not after, because unwinding a financing
structure that doesn't fit the filing is far harder than choosing the right
one up front.

## Real estate for residency versus real estate as an investment

Buying property in Paraguay because you like the numbers — rental yield,
appreciation, a place to eventually retire — is a completely different
exercise from buying property to qualify for the Investor Pass. The two can
be the same purchase, but only if you sequence it correctly. A property
that's a fine investment on a five-year horizon can still be the wrong
vehicle for a residency filing you want to submit in three months, if the
title work isn't finished yet.

We are not a brokerage and we don't sell property. What we do is take the

later.

## Timing, once the property is in order

Once title, valuation, and lien certificates are in hand, the real estate
route doesn't move any faster through the residency process itself than
other routes. The <Fact k="cedula.timeline" /> for the identity document
still applies, and permanent residency still carries its own
<Fact k="permanent.presence_rule" />. What real estate buys you is a cleaner
starting point, not a shortcut through the stages after it.

Worth knowing separately: owning property in Paraguay doesn't by itself
change what you owe in taxes elsewhere, and Paraguay's own
<Fact k="tax.territorial_rate" /> applies to income sourced here, not to
your residency status. We handle the immigration filing; get your own tax
advice on how a Paraguayan property fits your situation before you buy.

If your title is clean and your valuation holds up, real estate is one of
the more predictable routes into the Pass. If either of those isn't settled
yet, that's the work to do first, before anything gets filed.
