Most explanations of what delays an immigration case are generic enough to
apply to any country's program: "gather your documents early," "work with a
professional." True, but not useful. This is the specific version, built
around what actually slows an Investor Pass case down in practice, based on
the pattern of issues that come up repeatedly rather than a theoretical list.

## Route selection made too late, or made twice

The Investor Pass qualifies through more than one route — real estate, a
productive business, financial instruments, or a qualifying tourism-sector
investment. Each one has its own documentation shape and its own rhythm.
The single biggest source of lost time we see is not any one route being
slow — it is someone starting to gather documents for one route, changing
their mind partway through, and effectively restarting on a different one.

This usually happens because the route wasn't seriously evaluated against
the person's actual situation up front. Cash on hand versus financing,
comfort with property management, how liquid the investor wants to stay —
these should shape the route decision on day one, not surface as a reason to
switch three months in. If you have not settled on a route yet, our
[route finder](/route-finder) is a reasonable starting point, but the real
work is a conversation about your specific circumstances, not a quiz result.

## Source-of-funds documentation gathered reactively

Proving where investment capital came from is not a formality — it is
scrutinized, and it needs to hold together as a coherent story: income,
savings, a sale, an inheritance, whatever the actual source is. The mistake
we see most often is treating this as something to produce only once it is
asked for, rather than assembling it alongside the investment itself.

Bank statements that only cover a recent window, income documentation from
an employer who is slow to respond, funds that moved through more than one
account before reaching Paraguay — any of these turns a same-week request
into a month-long back-and-forth if it starts after the fact instead of
before.

## Documents that expire before the case is ready

Several of the personal documents an application depends on — the police
certificate being the clearest example — are only accepted within a certain
recency window. <Fact k="documents.police_certificate_validity" /> If a case
takes longer to assemble than expected on the investment side, a document
gathered early can quietly age out before filing, and nobody notices until
it is rejected. The fix is not complicated — track expiry dates against your
actual filing timeline rather than against the date you first requested the
document — but it is the kind of detail that is easy to lose track of across
a process with several moving parts.

## Family documentation treated as an afterthought

When a spouse, children, or other dependents are part of the case, their
paperwork is often the last thing gathered, on the assumption that it is
simpler than the investment side. Sometimes it is. Sometimes an apostilled
marriage certificate from a country with a slow apostille process, or a
consent letter from a parent who is not part of the application, ends up
taking longer than everything else combined. We go through this in more
detail in [bringing your family](/insights/bringing-your-family)
— the short version here is: settle who is being included and start their
documents early, in parallel with the investment paperwork, not after it.

## Banking friction

Opening the accounts needed to move and hold investment funds, and getting
those funds to actually land and be documented correctly, is a step that
looks simple on paper and routinely is not. International transfers get
flagged for additional review. Account opening can require an in-person step
or documents that themselves need to be legalized. None of this is unusual
for cross-border banking generally — it is just easy to underestimate if you
assume it will take the same day or two it might at a bank you already have
a relationship with.

## An investment structure that does not cleanly match the route

Occasionally the investment itself is real and sufficient in value, but
structured in a way that does not map cleanly onto what the chosen route
actually requires — a property held through an entity when the route expects
personal ownership, for instance, or funds split across accounts in a way
that obscures rather than demonstrates the source. This kind of mismatch
usually surfaces during review rather than before filing, which is the
worst time for it to surface. Confirming the structure against the specific
route's requirements before you invest, not after, avoids this entirely.

## What this list adds up to

None of these are about the government being slow. Government processing on
a complete, well-structured file moves at the pace described in
[how long the Investor Pass actually takes](/insights/how-long-it-actually-takes).
Almost every real delay we see traces back to something on the applicant's
side that could have started earlier or been sequenced differently — not
bad luck, and not bureaucracy, just preparation that happened reactively
instead of ahead of time.

The practical takeaway is not "be more organized" in the abstract. It is:
settle your route first, start source-of-funds and family documents in
parallel with the investment itself rather than after it, and track document
expiry against your real timeline rather than the date you first requested
each one. That alone accounts for most of the difference between a case that
moves smoothly and one that stalls repeatedly on the same avoidable issues.
