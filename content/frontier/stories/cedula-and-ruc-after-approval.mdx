Approval letters get treated like a finish line online, and that is one of the more
expensive misunderstandings we see. Residency approval is the start of a second, shorter
process — cédula, then RUC — and until both are in hand, most of what you actually moved
to Paraguay to do is still out of reach.

## What the approval letter actually gives you

The approval itself is a legal status, not a usable document. You cannot open a bank
account with it, sign a lease with it, or show it at most counters and have it mean
anything to the person behind them. What you can do with it is apply for the cédula,
which is the physical identity document that turns "approved for residency" into
something a bank, landlord, or government office actually recognizes on sight.

## Step one: the cédula

Cédula timing: <Fact k="cedula.timeline" />. It is the single document that does the most practical work of anything in
your file — it is what a bank asks for, what a landlord asks for, and what any
government office defaults to when it wants to confirm who you are. Until it is in
hand, you are technically a resident on paper but functionally still operating like a
visitor for almost everything day to day.

We file the cédula application as the next step immediately after approval rather than
leaving a gap where nothing is moving. That gap is exactly where cases drift — someone
gets the good news, takes a breath, and three months later realizes nobody actually
followed up on the next form.

## Step two: the RUC

Once the cédula is issued, the RUC — the tax identification number — is the next piece,
and it matters more than most people expect before they get here. You do not need to be
running a business to need one. Banks ask for it when opening an account. Some landlords
ask for it before signing a lease. Utility providers and other service accounts ask for
it too. Treating the RUC as something you will "deal with later if it comes up" is one of
the more common ways a new resident ends up making an extra trip to a government office
weeks after they thought they were done.

We register the RUC as part of the same filing sequence as the residency itself, rather
than as an afterthought, specifically to avoid that extra trip.

## What order actually unlocks what

The order matters because each document depends on the one before it, and skipping ahead
does not work the way people hope it will:

- **Residency approval** lets you apply for the cédula. It does not, by itself, open a
  bank account or let you sign most contracts.
- **The cédula** lets you apply for the RUC, and on its own is usually enough to satisfy
  a landlord or a basic service provider that wants proof of who you are.
- **The RUC**, alongside the cédula, is what most banks actually require for a full
  account — not a limited or restricted one, but one that receives transfers, holds a
  reasonable balance, and comes with a debit card that works the way you expect.

Working — whether formally employed or self-employed — and most business registration
steps also sit behind having both documents in place. If part of your plan involves
earning locally rather than living on income from home, this sequence is not optional
paperwork, it is the actual precondition.

## Why this trips people up

A lot of the online chatter around Paraguay residency stops at "get approved" as though
that is the whole story. It is not, and the honest version is more useful even though it
is less exciting: approval is the credential that lets you start the next two steps, not
the end of the process. Someone who assumes the approval letter itself opens doors is
the person who shows up to a bank branch, gets turned away, and is genuinely surprised
by it.
