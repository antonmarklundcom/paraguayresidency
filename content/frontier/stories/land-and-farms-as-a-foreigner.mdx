One of the recurring claims in "plan B" content about Paraguay is that foreigners can
buy land here with almost none of the restrictions found elsewhere in the region, and
that agricultural land in particular is inexpensive. Both of those claims are broadly
true. What gets left out is the part that actually determines whether a purchase turns
out well: title quality, and who is doing the checking.

## What foreigners can actually buy

Paraguay does not impose the kind of foreign-ownership restrictions on standard rural
or urban property that some neighbouring countries apply, particularly around border
zones. You do not need to be a resident, let alone a citizen, to hold title to land or a
house here. This is a real point in Paraguay's favor relative to markets where
foreign buyers are funneled into leaseholds or corporate structures just to hold
property.

That said, "no legal barrier to buying" and "safe to buy without checking" are two
different statements, and the gap between them is where problems actually happen.

## Why land here is inexpensive

Paraguay's land prices, especially for agricultural parcels outside the immediate
vicinity of Asunción and the border cities, are low by the standards of North America,
Europe, or even most of the rest of South America. Large-scale soy and cattle operations
have driven up prices in the most productive zones, but plenty of land outside those
specific belts remains genuinely affordable. This is one of the more concrete, checkable
claims in the "plan B" pitch, and it holds up.

## Where the risk actually lives: title and boundaries

Land records outside the major cities are not always as clean or as digitized as buyers
from North America or Western Europe expect. Boundary disputes, overlapping claims, and
inconsistent survey records are real and documented issues in parts of rural Paraguay,
and they are exactly the kind of problem that does not show up until you try to sell,
develop, or fence the property.

This is not a reason to avoid buying land here. It is a reason to treat title
verification as non-negotiable rather than a formality. A proper check means an
independent local lawyer — not the seller's lawyer, not the broker's in-house counsel —
pulling the actual registry records, confirming the chain of title, and physically
verifying boundaries against the survey, before any money changes hands.

## The eastern region versus the Chaco

Paraguay splits roughly into the eastern region, where most of the population,
infrastructure and agricultural activity sit, and the Chaco, a vast, sparsely populated
plain to the west that makes up more than half the country's land area by size but a
small fraction of its population. Land in the Chaco is generally cheaper still than in
the east, and cattle ranching dominates it, but road access, water availability and
distance from services are real constraints that price alone does not capture. The
eastern region, particularly the areas around Ciudad del Este, Encarnación and the
agricultural belt further south, carries higher prices precisely because the
infrastructure and market access that make land productive are already in place. Buyers
drawn purely by the lowest headline price per hectare in the Chaco should weigh what it
actually costs to make that land usable against what a smaller, better-connected parcel
in the east would cost instead.

## Financing and how purchases are typically structured

Foreign buyers without an existing Paraguayan credit history should not expect local
bank financing on the terms they might get at home; most purchases by foreigners here
are made in cash or through financing arranged in the buyer's home country against
other assets. This is worth planning for before you fall in love with a specific
property, since financing timelines that assume a straightforward local mortgage
process tend to be the first assumption that turns out wrong.

## Farms and agricultural operations specifically

If the interest is a working farm rather than a residential lot, the calculus shifts
again. Productive agricultural land, water access, road access for equipment and
harvest transport, and proximity to the agro-export infrastructure around cities like
Ciudad del Este and Encarnación all matter more than raw acreage price. A cheap parcel
with no usable road access is not actually cheap once you account for what it costs to
make it productive.
