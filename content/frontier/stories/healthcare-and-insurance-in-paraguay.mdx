Healthcare rarely gets the same attention as tax and residency requirements in
plan-B content, which is odd, because it is one of the things that actually determines
day-to-day quality of life if you spend meaningful time here. The honest picture has a
real split in it: Asunción and the interior are not the same story.

## Asunción: genuinely solid, and inexpensive

Private hospitals and clinics in Asunción are generally well-regarded, with
internationally trained doctors, modern equipment in the major private hospitals, and
costs that are a fraction of equivalent care in the US, Canada, the UK or Australia. For
routine care, specialist consultations, and most procedures, residents — foreign and
local alike — who use the private system report a good experience. This is one of the
more concrete, checkable advantages of a Paraguay base, not just a marketing claim.

## Outside Asunción: a real drop-off

The picture changes meaningfully once you leave the capital. Smaller cities and rural
areas have far fewer specialists, less equipment, and in some cases meaningfully longer
travel times to serious care. This is not unique to Paraguay — it is common across much
of the region — but it is worth stating plainly rather than letting a rosy Asunción
picture stand in for the whole country. If your plan involves land or a farm outside the
capital (see [buying land and farms as a foreigner](/stories/land-and-farms-as-a-foreigner)),
proximity to serious medical care is a genuine factor worth weighing, not an
afterthought.

## The public system

Paraguay has a public healthcare system, and residents generally have access to it. In
practice, most foreign residents who can afford private insurance use the private
system for anything beyond a true emergency, both for speed and for the language and
service-quality differences. This mirrors how most locals with the means to do so
approach it as well — it is not a foreigner-specific workaround.

## How residents typically handle insurance

There are three common approaches: local private health insurance, which is generally
inexpensive relative to comparable coverage in North America or Europe; international
health insurance carried over from elsewhere, common among people who split time
between countries; or paying out of pocket for routine care given how low private
costs are, with insurance reserved for anything serious. Which of these fits depends
heavily on your age, health situation, and how much time you actually spend in-country
— exactly the kind of detail worth discussing directly rather than generalizing.

## What a typical visit looks like

For routine care in Asunción, the experience is closer to a straightforward
walk-in-and-be-seen model than the multi-week specialist waits common in some public
health systems elsewhere. Private clinics generally see patients quickly, and many
doctors trained or did fellowships abroad, particularly in the US or Europe, which
shows up in how consultations are conducted even when the language of the visit is
Spanish or Guaraní. English-speaking doctors are findable in the capital, though not
guaranteed at every clinic, so confirming language ahead of an appointment is worth the
extra call if it matters to you.

## Prescription medication and pharmacies

Pharmacies in Asunción are well stocked and many common medications are available
without the prior-authorization friction some residents are used to from insurance-heavy
systems elsewhere. This is a genuine convenience for people managing an ongoing
prescription, though it also means bringing your own medical history and current
prescriptions documented in a form a local doctor can use is worth doing before you
need it urgently rather than after.

## What this means for a plan-B decision

If your Paraguay residency is genuinely a reserve card you rarely use, healthcare
access may matter less to your decision than it would to someone planning a full move.
If you are seriously weighing spending real time here — not just holding a document —
understanding where you would actually be spending that time, and what care looks like
there specifically, matters more than the country-level averages any single article can
give you.

## Dental and elective care

Dental work and other elective procedures are a specific case worth separating out:
private dental care in Asunción is generally both accessible and inexpensive relative
to the US, Canada, the UK or Australia, and it is common for residents — and even some
non-residents visiting specifically for this — to handle dental work here rather than
at home. The same private-hospital quality that applies to general medical care
generally extends to dental and elective procedures, though as with any elective
treatment anywhere, checking a specific provider's credentials and reputation directly
rather than assuming quality by location is the right level of diligence.

## Emergency care and evacuation coverage

For anyone genuinely splitting time between Paraguay and elsewhere, it is worth asking
specifically whether an international policy includes medical evacuation coverage, not
just treatment cost coverage. Serious emergencies in areas far from Asunción's private
hospitals can mean a transfer is the difference between a good outcome and a bad one,
and that is a different kind of coverage than a standard travel-medical policy provides
by default. It is a detail insurance brokers do not always lead with unless asked.
