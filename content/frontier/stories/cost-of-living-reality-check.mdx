Most of what gets written about cost of living in Paraguay for Americans falls into one
of two camps: "everything is basically free" or a vague hand-wave that says nothing
useful. Neither helps someone actually deciding whether to move. This is our attempt at
the honest middle version, built from what we see clients actually spend once they are
here.

## The assumption people bring with them

Coming from the US or Canada, the instinct is to take a US or Canadian monthly budget,
apply a rough discount, and assume that is the Paraguay number. That instinct is not
crazy — <Fact k="costofliving.overview" /> — but it breaks down category by category in
ways that matter for planning, and it breaks down worse the more someone assumes a fully
American lifestyle transplanted unchanged into a Paraguayan city.

## Rent: the category with the biggest gap

Rent is where the difference between home and here is most dramatic, and also where
people most often either overestimate the savings or underestimate how much Asunción
itself varies by neighborhood. Rental costs: <Fact k="costofliving.rent" />. A modest
apartment in a good area of Asunción and a comparable one in a smaller city or the
interior are not the same price, and neither is automatically "cheap" just because it is
in Paraguay — but both sit well under what the same standard of living costs in most US
or Canadian metro areas.

The mistake we see most often is someone pricing a listing off a single site, assuming
that number represents the whole market, and then being surprised either way — pleasantly
when a better place costs less than expected, or unpleasantly when a well-located
apartment in a popular part of the capital costs more than the "Paraguay is cheap"
headline implied.

## Groceries and eating out

Day-to-day spending is the category where the gap narrows the most, and it is worth
saying plainly rather than letting the rent number set expectations for everything else:
<Fact k="costofliving.groceries" />. Imported goods and anything marketed toward
expats specifically cost more than the local equivalent, sometimes close to what you
would pay at home. Shopping and eating the way locals do — local markets, seasonal
produce, Paraguayan and regional restaurants rather than imported-brand grocery aisles —
is where the real savings show up. Someone who insists on recreating an identical
American shopping cart and expects Paraguay pricing on it will be disappointed.

## Healthcare: better than assumed, not identical to home

This is the category where American and Canadian assumptions run in opposite,
equally wrong directions. Some people assume healthcare here is a downgrade not worth
the savings elsewhere; others assume it is functionally the same system at a fraction of
the price with no differences at all. Neither is quite right. Private healthcare in and
around Asunción is generally solid, and out-of-pocket costs for consultations,
procedures, and private insurance run well below equivalent US costs in particular —
Canada's public system is a different comparison, since routine care there is not
typically an out-of-pocket cost to begin with. What actually matters for your own
decision is provider quality for your specific situation, not a single generic
comparison, which is exactly the kind of question worth a real conversation rather than
a blog-post average.

## What does not get cheaper

Not everything drops. Imported electronics, imported cars, and anything built around a
global brand rather than a local or regional supply chain tend to cost the same or more
than at home, sometimes noticeably more once import costs are factored in. Anyone
budgeting on the assumption that literally every line item shrinks is setting up a
budget that will not survive contact with an actual shopping trip.

## The honest range, and why we will not compress it into one number

We are deliberately not printing a single "you can live on $X a month" figure here,
because that number depends enormously on which city, what kind of housing, and what
lifestyle someone actually wants — a retiree in a quiet interior town and a remote
worker wanting a modern apartment in central Asunción are not budgeting for the same
life, even though both are "living in Paraguay." A number precise enough to look
trustworthy and generic enough to apply to everyone does not exist, and we would rather
walk through your actual numbers on a call than hand you one that looks authoritative
and turns out wrong for your situation specifically.
