Short answer: yes, Americans can retire in Paraguay on Social Security, and a fair number do. The benefit keeps arriving, the residency process treats a pension as legitimate income, and the monthly budget is modest by US standards.

The catch comes in three parts. Medicare mostly stays behind when you leave. The IRS doesn't. And healthcare outside Asunción is thin. If you plan around all three before you go, Paraguay is a practical place to retire. If you plan around the monthly budget alone, you'll be surprised.

## Can Americans retire in Paraguay?

Yes. There is no separate "retiree visa." You go through the same two steps as everyone else. First comes temporary residency, which lasts <Fact k="temporary.duration" />. Then you convert to permanent residency, which for 2026 files asks for <Fact k="solvency.requirement" />. Retirees are one of the recognized applicant categories, so a pension is the natural way to qualify.

You'll also need the US document set: an FBI background check, a birth certificate and, if you're married, a marriage certificate, each apostilled and translated. That part is slow, and it happens at home. [The US document chain](/stories/us-document-chain-before-paraguay) walks through it.

Getting in to start the process is easy for now: <Fact k="entry.visa_exemption_us_ca_au" />.

## How does Social Security reach you in Paraguay?

The Social Security Administration pays retirement benefits to US citizens living in most countries. The restricted list is short (Cuba and North Korea are the best known), and Paraguay isn't on it. Check your own case with the SSA's [Payments Abroad Screening Tool](https://www.ssa.gov/international/payments_outsideUS.html) before you move.

Three practical points the brochures skip:

- **Keep your address current with the SSA.** Beneficiaries abroad get periodic questionnaires. Ignore one and your payments can be suspended until you answer.
- **Decide where the money lands.** Many retirees keep a US bank account for the deposit and move money to Paraguay as they need it. Check the SSA's international direct deposit list for your country options, and ask your US bank how it treats customers with a foreign address.
- **SSI is different.** Supplemental Security Income generally stops when you live outside the US. Retirement benefits are the ones that travel.

None of this is Paraguay's rule to change. It's between you and the SSA, so confirm your own situation with them directly.

## The Medicare gap

This is the part retirees most often get wrong. Medicare generally doesn't pay for care outside the United States. In Paraguay you're a private patient, and you pay the clinic or your insurer does.

Private care in Asunción is solid by regional standards, with internationally trained doctors and modern equipment in the main private hospitals. The cost is a fraction of US prices. Outside the capital, specialists and equipment drop off quickly. That matters more at seventy than at forty. [Healthcare and insurance in Paraguay](/stories/healthcare-and-insurance-in-paraguay) covers the split in detail.

Two decisions to make before you leave:

1. **Local or international insurance.** Local plans are cheaper and work well in Asunción. International plans cost more and can cover you on trips home. Many local plans have age limits for new members or waiting periods for pre-existing conditions, so ask before you commit.
2. **Medicare Part B.** Some retirees keep paying for it so they're covered on visits and if they ever move back. Others drop it. Part B has late-enrollment penalties if you drop it and rejoin later, so read medicare.gov before you decide.

**The catch:** if your plan is "fly home for anything serious," price out the flight, the time and the insurance that covers the gap. It's a plan, but it isn't free.

## Will Paraguay tax your Social Security?

Paraguay's side is the easy part. The system is territorial: <Fact k="tax.foreign_income_treatment" />. Social Security paid from the US is foreign-source income for Paraguay. If you earn locally, say rent from a Paraguayan apartment, that income is taxed at <Fact k="tax.territorial_rate" />.

The US side doesn't change because you moved. The US taxes its citizens on worldwide income, and Social Security benefits can be partly taxable depending on your total income. The foreign earned income exclusion covers earned income, so it doesn't shelter pensions or Social Security. There's also no tax treaty to fall back on: Paraguay has <Fact k="tax.double_tax_treaties" />. And some states keep taxing former residents who haven't clearly cut ties. Talk to a US-qualified tax professional before you set up your move, not after the first April abroad. Our [US taxes piece](/stories/us-taxes-while-holding-paraguay-residency) covers the filing basics.

## The presence rule and the grandkids

Retirees who split the year between Paraguay and family in the US are exactly who the presence rules were written for. Temporary residency requires <Fact k="temporary.presence_rule" />. Permanent residency requires <Fact k="permanent.presence_rule" />.

In practice, a retiree who lives in Paraguay and flies home for a few months each year has nothing to worry about. The risk is the opposite pattern: someone who moves back to be near family, keeps the card "just in case" and lets years slip by. ## Who Paraguay suits, and who it doesn't

| Suits well | Suits poorly |
|---|---|
| Retirees who want a low cost base and a slow pace | Anyone who needs regular specialist care outside Asunción |
| People comfortable learning some Spanish | People who want a large English-speaking retiree community |
| Retirees with a documented pension or Social Security | Anyone counting on Medicare for day-to-day care |
| People who travel home a few times a year | People who want direct flights to the US |
