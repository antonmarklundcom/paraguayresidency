A residency card gets repeated online as the thing that "gives you a Paraguay bank
account," as though the two are the same document. They are not. Banking is a separate
process with its own requirements, and skipping past that detail is one of the more
common ways a plan-B case turns into a frustrating first month.

## What actually opens an account

Paraguayan banks generally want three things from a new resident: a valid residency
document (the cédula, not just proof that an application is in progress), a RUC — the
tax identification number — and proof of address. None of these arrive automatically
the moment residency is approved; they are separate steps that follow it, which is why
we register your RUC alongside your residency filing rather than leaving it for later.

Some banks will work with residency-in-progress or a passport alone for limited
products, but the account that actually functions the way you want — receiving
transfers, holding balances in more than one currency, a debit card that works
smoothly — reliably requires the completed cédula and RUC.

## Why the RUC matters more than people expect

The RUC is easy to dismiss if you are not planning to run a business here, but banks,
landlords and some service providers ask for one regardless of whether you are
self-employed. Registering it during your residency filing, rather than as an
afterthought once you discover you need it, is one of the small sequencing decisions
that saves an extra trip to a government office later.

## What to expect at the bank itself

New-resident banking here is a genuinely in-person process. Expect at least one branch
visit, physical document verification, and account terms explained by a human rather
than an app onboarding flow. This is not unique to Paraguay, but it does surprise
people used to opening accounts remotely elsewhere. Bringing your cédula, RUC
certificate, and proof of address to the appointment covers most of what gets asked.

## Currency and what accounts actually hold

Paraguay's currency is the guaraní, and most banks offer accounts that can hold both
guaraníes and US dollars, which matters if your income or savings are dollar-denominated
and you would rather not convert everything on day one. Ask specifically about
multi-currency accounts when you have the conversation — not every branch leads with
that option unprompted.
