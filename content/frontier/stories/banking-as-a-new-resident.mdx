A residency card gets repeated online as the thing that "gives you a Paraguay bank
account," as though the two are the same document. They are not. Banking is a separate
process with its own requirements, and skipping past that detail is one of the more
common ways a plan-B case turns into a frustrating first month.

## What actually opens an account

Paraguayan banks generally want three things from a new resident: a valid residency
document (the cédula, not just proof that an application is in progress), a RUC — the
tax identification number — and proof of address. None of these arrive automatically
the moment residency is approved; they are separate steps that follow it, which is why
we register your RUC alongside your residency filing rather than leaving it for later.

Some banks will work with residency-in-progress or a passport alone for limited
products, but the account that actually functions the way you want — receiving
transfers, holding balances in more than one currency, a debit card that works
smoothly — reliably requires the completed cédula and RUC.

## Why the RUC matters more than people expect

The RUC is easy to dismiss if you are not planning to run a business here, but banks,
landlords and some service providers ask for one regardless of whether you are
self-employed. Registering it during your residency filing, rather than as an
afterthought once you discover you need it, is one of the small sequencing decisions
that saves an extra trip to a government office later.

## What to expect at the bank itself

New-resident banking here is a genuinely in-person process. Expect at least one branch
visit, physical document verification, and account terms explained by a human rather
than an app onboarding flow. This is not unique to Paraguay, but it does surprise
people used to opening accounts remotely elsewhere. Bringing your cédula, RUC
certificate, and proof of address to the appointment covers most of what gets asked.

## Currency and what accounts actually hold

Paraguay's currency is the guaraní, and most banks offer accounts that can hold both
guaraníes and US dollars, which matters if your income or savings are dollar-denominated
and you would rather not convert everything on day one. Ask specifically about
multi-currency accounts when you have the conversation — not every branch leads with
that option unprompted.

## Digital and fintech alternatives, and their limits

Some new residents lean on international fintech accounts and cards for day-to-day
spending before or alongside opening a local bank account, and that can genuinely
bridge the gap during the weeks between arriving and completing residency paperwork.
The limit shows up quickly, though: fintech accounts rarely satisfy the "proof of local
banking" requests that come up for local contracts, some rental agreements, or larger
transactions, and they do not substitute for the RUC when a bank or counterparty
specifically asks for one. Treat them as a bridge, not a replacement, for a proper
local account.

## Wiring money in

However you plan to fund the account initially, ask about incoming wire requirements
before you send anything — some banks want the source and purpose of larger incoming
transfers documented, and finding that out after a wire is already in transit is a
worse position than confirming it beforehand. This is a small step that saves a real
delay, and it is exactly the kind of practical detail that tends to fall between
"legal requirement" and "obvious," which is why it belongs here rather than assumed.

## Statements, taxes and paper trails

Once an account is open, keep the habit of downloading statements periodically rather
than assuming online access will always cover a long enough history when you need it —
whether for a future visa application elsewhere, a tax filing at home, or simply your
own records. This is unglamorous advice, and it is also the kind of thing that costs
nothing to do proactively and can cost real time to reconstruct after the fact if you
skip it for a year and then need six months of transaction history in a hurry.

## Choosing a bank, practically

Not every bank markets itself to foreign residents equally, and the difference shows up
in how smoothly the onboarding conversation goes. Some branches, particularly in areas
of Asunción with more foreign residents, handle the process routinely; others may treat
a foreign passport and a fresh cédula as an unusual case requiring extra sign-off. This
is one of the more useful things to ask us directly rather than guess at from a
brochure — which specific banks we have seen work smoothly for recent foreign
residents, and which paperwork to bring on the first visit so the appointment does not
need a second trip.
