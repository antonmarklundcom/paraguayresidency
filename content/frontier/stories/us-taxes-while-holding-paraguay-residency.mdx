Here is the uncomfortable sentence first. If you are a US citizen, getting Paraguay residency doesn't change your US tax obligations. Paraguay's side is friendly to foreign income, because <Fact k="tax.foreign_income_treatment" />. The US system follows your passport, not your address. You live under both.

Nothing below is tax advice. It's a map of which questions belong to which country, so you walk into a meeting with a US-qualified tax professional knowing what to ask.

## What Paraguay taxes

Paraguay taxes territorially: <Fact k="tax.foreign_income_treatment" />. Your US salary for remote work, US investment income and US pensions are, from Paraguay's side, foreign-source.

Income sourced in Paraguay is different. Local wages, fees from local clients, rent from a Paraguayan apartment and profit from a local business are taxed at <Fact k="tax.territorial_rate" />. Personal income runs in brackets: <Fact k="tax.irp_brackets" />. If you register a RUC, it can come with filing duties even in quiet years, so ask your accountant which obligations yours carries. [Our tax page](/tax) covers the RUC.

## Why the IRS still taxes you

The United States is one of very few countries that taxes by citizenship. You file a federal return every year, wherever you live, and report worldwide income. A Paraguayan residency card, a cédula or a Paraguayan tax residency certificate doesn't change that.

There's also no treaty to soften it. Paraguay has <Fact k="tax.double_tax_treaties" />. Where treaties exist, they settle tie-breaker questions and pension rules. Here, US domestic law does all the work.

State tax is a separate trap. Some states keep treating you as a resident if you haven't clearly cut ties: a home, a driver's license, voter registration. Ask about your state specifically.

## The foreign earned income exclusion

The foreign earned income exclusion (FEIE) lets qualifying Americans abroad exclude a capped amount of *earned* income, meaning wages and self-employment income, from US income tax. Three things to know:

- **It has its own tests.** You need a tax home abroad and must pass either the bona fide residence test or a day-count physical presence test. A Paraguayan residency card can support a bona fide residence claim. On its own it isn't the test.
- **It doesn't cover passive income.** Pensions, Social Security, dividends, interest and capital gains stay fully in the US system.
- **It doesn't remove self-employment tax.** Self-employed Americans usually still owe US Social Security tax, and there is no US–Paraguay social security agreement to change that.

**The catch:** a card held in reserve while you live in Ohio does nothing for the FEIE. The exclusion follows where you actually live and work.

## The foreign tax credit

If you pay Paraguayan tax on Paraguayan income, the US foreign tax credit may let you offset it against US tax on the same income. Because Paraguay's rate on local income is <Fact k="tax.territorial_rate" />, the credit often covers less than people expect, and the US may still collect a top-up. How the credit, the FEIE and your income mix interact is exactly what you pay a professional to model.

## FBAR and FATCA: reporting, not tax

This is where Americans abroad get hurt, usually through paperwork rather than tax.

- **FBAR.** If your foreign accounts together pass a reporting threshold at any point in the year, you file a separate report with FinCEN. That includes a Paraguayan checking account plus a savings account.
- **FATCA (Form 8938).** A second, overlapping report of foreign financial assets filed with your return, with its own, higher thresholds.
- **At the bank.** Paraguayan banks report US account holders under FATCA and will ask you for US tax forms when you open an account. See [banking as a new resident](/stories/banking-as-a-new-resident).

The penalties for missing these reports can exceed any tax you'd owe. Set up the reporting when you open the account, not three years later.

## A Paraguayan company?

A local company can make sense for a real local business. As a US tax play it rarely works the way forum posts suggest. US owners of foreign corporations face information returns and anti-deferral rules. Paraguay's own rates still apply to local profits: <Fact k="tax.territorial_rate" /> for individuals, and <Fact k="tax.ire_rate" /> for companies. Get the structure reviewed by a US-qualified tax professional before you register anything.

## Who taxes what

| Income | Paraguay | United States |
|---|---|---|
| US wages or clients, remote | <Fact k="tax.foreign_income_treatment" /> | Taxable; the FEIE may apply if you live abroad |
| US pension or Social Security | <Fact k="tax.foreign_income_treatment" /> | Taxable under US rules; the FEIE doesn't apply |
| US dividends and interest | <Fact k="tax.foreign_income_treatment" /> | Taxable; the FEIE doesn't apply |
| Paraguayan wages or rent | <Fact k="tax.territorial_rate" /> | Taxable; the foreign tax credit may offset it |

Separately, a Paraguayan bank account counts toward the US FBAR and FATCA reporting thresholds, whatever it holds.

## What a Paraguay plan B changes, and what it doesn't

It gives you a legal home abroad, a local ID, a RUC and a bank account. On Paraguay's side, <Fact k="tax.foreign_income_treatment" />. It doesn't end US filing. It doesn't make passive income excludable. It doesn't create a treaty that isn't there. And giving up US citizenship is a separate decision with its own US exit rules. It's far outside what a residency card does.
