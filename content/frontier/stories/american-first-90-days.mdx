Most of what gets written about Paraguay residency online is either a sales pitch or a
complaint thread. Neither tells you what the first three months of an actual case look
like for an American applicant. This is that account, generalized across the cases we
run every week — not a single client's story, but the shape almost every one of them
follows.

## Week 1–2: the call and the checklist

The case starts with a call, not a form. We ask what you actually want out of the
residency — a card held in reserve, a real move, something in between — because that
answer changes what we recommend and how we sequence your documents. From there you get
a checklist built for a US passport specifically, not a generic international one.

For Americans, the document that sets the pace is almost always the FBI background
check. It has to be requested, processed, and then apostilled through the US State
Department's authentication process, and that chain alone can take several weeks before
anything else can move. If there is one thing worth doing in week one, it is starting
that request the same day you decide to proceed — see [the process page](/process) for
the full nationality-by-nationality breakdown.

## Week 3–6: legalisation, running in parallel

While the FBI check is in transit, your birth certificate (and marriage certificate, if
you are filing with a spouse) go through their own apostille and sworn-translation
chain. None of this happens in Paraguay — it happens at home, through your state's
Secretary of State office and then a certified translator. We track all of it so
nothing arrives out of order and nothing expires sitting in a drawer waiting on another
document.

This stretch is the part that surprises people the most: there is very little for you
to actively do. Documents move through offices on their own schedule, and the honest
answer to "can we speed this up" is usually no. What we can do — and what actually
matters — is make sure nothing sits idle because someone forgot to request the next
piece.

## Week 7–10: filing and the Asunción appointments

Once your documents are complete and legalised, we schedule the actual filing
appointments in Asunción. This is the part that requires your physical presence — not
the whole 90 days, just these specific visits. For a straightforward single-applicant
case, this typically means two to three appointments spread across a couple of weeks:
submitting the application, biometrics, and any follow-up the immigration office
requests.

If you are filing with family, we schedule everyone together wherever the office
allows it, so you are not making separate trips for a spouse and children.

## Week 11–13: approval and the cédula

Residency approval is followed by the cédula de identidad application, which is
generally <Fact k="cedula.timeline" /> once residency itself is approved. This is the
document that actually lets you function locally — open accounts, sign contracts, prove
your status — and it is worth getting even if your plan is to hold the residency in
reserve rather than live here full time.

## What actually slows a case down

Across the cases we run, the same handful of things account for almost every delay, and
none of them are exotic. A name that appears slightly differently across documents — a
middle name present on a birth certificate but missing from a passport, for instance —
can stall an appointment while it gets clarified. A sworn translation done by a
translator not recognized for the specific document type has to be redone. An apostille
requested from the wrong office, because a state or federal jurisdiction question was
answered incorrectly, sends you back to the start of that one document's chain. None of
these are Paraguay-side problems; they are home-country paperwork issues that surface
once a foreign document meets a foreign bureaucracy's expectations. We flag likely
mismatches during the checklist stage specifically to keep them from turning into a
mid-process delay.

## What we are not telling you

We are not telling you this always runs exactly 90 days. It is a realistic window for a
clean, well-sequenced case, and the biggest variable is how quickly your home-country
offices process the FBI check and apostilles — which is outside anyone's control on
this end. We are also not telling you that once you have the card, you never have to
think about Paraguay again: permanent residency in particular carries a real presence
requirement, which is exactly why [the presence rules deserve their own honest
explanation](/stories/the-presence-rules-nobody-explains) rather than a footnote here.

## What comes after residency

A residency card by itself does not open a bank account — that is a separate process
with its own document requirements, and worth understanding before you assume the two
happen automatically together. See [banking as a new
resident](/stories/banking-as-a-new-resident) for what that actually involves.
