People ask us this before almost anything else about the process itself: once
the paperwork is done, what does it actually cost to live here? The honest
answer is that Asunción is genuinely inexpensive compared to most Western
capitals, but "cheap" isn't the right word either. Some things cost less than
you'd expect. A few cost more. And a fair amount depends on choices you make
in your first few months, before you know the city well enough to make them
efficiently.

We're not going to quote you a monthly budget number here. Cost-of-living
figures shift with the exchange rate, the season, and the neighborhood, and a
number that's accurate today reads wrong in six months. What we can give you
is the shape of where your money goes, so you can build your own estimate
instead of trusting someone else's spreadsheet.

## Housing: renting vs buying

Most people who move here rent for at least the first year, and we'd
recommend it even if you're planning to buy eventually. Asunción's
neighborhoods vary more than the city's size suggests. Areas like Villa Morra,
Carmelitas, and parts of Recoleta carry a real premium — new buildings,
elevators, generators for the power cuts, walking distance to the better
restaurants and gyms. Move a few kilometers out toward Sajonia or the areas
past the Costanera and rents drop noticeably for a similar amount of space,
though you trade off some walkability and you'll want a car sooner.

Buying is straightforward on paper — foreigners can own property here without
special permission — but it's a slower process than renting, and it ties you
to a neighborhood before you've actually lived in the city. We generally tell
clients: rent for a year, learn which pockets of Asunción suit how you
actually live, then buy if you still want to. Renting month to month or on a
short lease is common and landlords are used to it.

## Groceries and eating out

Local markets and Paraguayan staples — beef, mandioca, seasonal produce — are
inexpensive relative to imported goods. If you cook mostly local ingredients
and shop at the ferias rather than the supermarket chains, your grocery bill
stays low. The moment you start wanting imported cheese, specific wines, or
brands you're used to from home, prices climb fast, because most of that
comes in through Argentina or Brazil and carries the cost of getting it here.

Eating out is one of the genuine pleasant surprises. A decent parrilla meal or
a casual lunch costs a fraction of the equivalent in a European or North
American city, and even the better restaurants in Villa Morra are reasonable
by comparison. Coffee shop culture has grown a lot in the last few years, and
that end of the market prices closer to what you'd expect anywhere.

## Utilities

Electricity, water, and internet are billed separately and none of them is
usually the line item that surprises new residents. What does surprise people
is air conditioning load in the hot months — Asunción summers are long and
genuinely hot, and running AC across a house for several months adds up more
than most newcomers budget for in year one. Power cuts happen, more in some
neighborhoods than others, which is part of why the pricier buildings with
their own generators justify the premium for some people and not for others.

## Transport: car, bus, or rideshare

Asunción's public bus system covers the city but isn't fast, isn't always on
a predictable schedule, and isn't the way most expats get around day to day.
Rideshare apps work and are inexpensive for occasional trips, so plenty of
people go months without a car if they live centrally and don't have a daily
commute outside walking distance.

That said, once you have kids in school, a job across town, or you want to
get out of the city on weekends — and a lot of people do, Paraguay's
interior and the river towns are worth seeing — a car stops being optional.
Used cars are common and reasonably priced by regional standards; new cars
carry import costs that push prices up. Traffic in the center gets bad at
rush hour but nothing like a major regional capital.

## Healthcare and insurance

Private healthcare in Asunción is good, and it's one of the reasons people
who've lived in several countries in the region rate Paraguay well for
quality of life. Private clinics and hospitals are modern, English is spoken
by a reasonable share of specialists, and appointment wait times are short
compared to what many residents were used to at home.

Most residents carry a local private health plan rather than relying on
public healthcare, and the premium for a family plan is meaningfully higher
than for a single adult — the usual pattern anywhere, just worth planning for
before you move rather than after. If you're bringing an existing
international policy, check whether it actually covers care inside Paraguay
before you assume it does; several widely sold expat policies exclude South
America or price it separately.

## Singles vs families: how the math changes

A single person renting a one-bedroom in a central neighborhood, eating a mix
of local and imported food, and using rideshare instead of owning a car has
the lowest and most flexible cost base — you can trade space and location for
savings pretty easily.

A family changes almost every line. You'll likely want more space, which
usually means either a pricier central apartment or a larger place further
out plus a car. School costs vary enormously between the international
schools and the local private options, and healthcare for a family plan costs
more per person than it does for a single adult on their own plan. None of
this makes Asunción expensive by international standards — it's still
markedly cheaper than raising a family in most of the cities people are
moving from — but the gap between a single person's budget and a family's is
wider here than the headline "low cost of living" suggests.
