Almost every foreign resident hits the same wall in their first few months
here: you can't get a proper local bank account without residency in hand,
and you can't feel settled without a bank account. It's not a Paraguay-specific
problem — most countries make banks the gatekeepers of who counts as a real
resident — but it catches people off guard because they assumed a passport
and an address would be enough.

## Why residency comes first

Paraguayan banks are required to know who they're banking, and a tourist
without a residency file doesn't give them much to verify against. In
practice this means banks want to see that your temporary or permanent
residency is either approved or far enough along that it's not a question
mark. Some banks will start the paperwork alongside a residency application
that's clearly moving, but the account isn't usually functional — cards
issued, transfers enabled — until the residency itself is in hand.

There are workarounds people talk about: a savings-only product available to
tourists, a business account opened through a company structure, an
international bank's Paraguay branch that's a little more flexible with
expats. We'll be honest about these rather than oversell them — they tend to
come with limits on what you can actually do with the account, and none of
them replace a normal resident account once you're eligible for one. If
you're weighing whether to rush a workaround or just wait the extra weeks or
months for residency to clear, waiting is usually the less frustrating path.

## What the bank asks for

Once your residency is sorted, the document list is fairly standard: your
passport, proof of address here, and your cédula, which is the identity card
issued once your residency is approved. <Fact k="cedula.timeline" />, so the
account application tends to follow shortly after the residency approval
itself rather than running in parallel with it.

Banks will also usually ask about the source of your funds — where the money
you're depositing comes from — and some ask for a RUC, Paraguay's tax
identification number, before they'll finish setting up the account. A few
banks issue debit cards and basic transfers with less friction than others,
but almost all of them will want you in a branch at least once, in person,
to sign and verify identity. Very little of this is done remotely, which
surprises people used to opening accounts online.

## Local banks vs international-friendly options

Paraguay has a mix of long-established local banks and a smaller set of
banks that are more used to dealing with foreign clients and English-language
paperwork. The local banks tend to have the widest branch networks and the
most straightforward peso-denominated accounts for everyday spending — rent,
utilities, groceries. The more international-friendly options are often
easier to deal with if your Spanish isn't strong yet, or if you expect to
hold both local currency and US dollars in the same relationship, but they
aren't necessarily faster to open, and some carry higher minimum balances.

Neither category is uniformly "the best" one — it depends on where you'll
actually be spending day to day, whether you need dollar accounts alongside
guaraní ones, and how comfortable you are handling paperwork in Spanish
without help. This is one of the things worth asking about on a call rather
than guessing from a list, since the right answer depends on your specific
situation.

## The RUC and how it connects to tax residency

A RUC does more than unlock a bank account — it's the number Paraguay's tax
authority uses to track you, and it's tied into how your tax residency here
actually works. Paraguay taxes on a territorial basis, meaning
<Fact k="tax.territorial_rate" />, and having a RUC is part of what makes that
status concrete rather than theoretical. If you're weighing what tax
residency actually changes for you — not just banking, but how your income
gets treated — that's a separate question from opening an account, and it's
worth working through properly rather than assuming. Our [tax residency
service](/residency/tax-residency) page covers what's actually involved, and
it's a conversation we'd rather have with you directly than leave to a
generic article, because so much depends on where your income comes from.

## Where people get frustrated

The in-person requirement is the most common complaint. You can't do this by
email from another country, and you often can't do it in a single visit
either — banks in Paraguay will sometimes ask you to come back with an
additional document you didn't know you needed, which is annoying but not
unusual for the region. Language is the second common friction point: branch
staff outside the more international-facing banks may not do business
banking in English, so bringing someone who can translate, or working with
whoever is handling your residency file, saves a repeat trip.

Minimum deposits and account-keeping fees also catch people out. They're not
dramatic by international standards, but they're rarely advertised clearly
up front, and some accounts that look free on paper carry a small monthly
fee once you're actually using them. None of this is unusual for someone
opening a first account in a new country — it's just not what people expect
after hearing Paraguay described as simple across the board.

## Getting the order right

The practical sequence is: residency approved, cédula in hand, RUC applied
for, then the bank account. Trying to skip ahead of that order tends to cost
more time than it saves. If you're not yet sure which residency route gets
you here fastest — temporary, permanent, or the investor route — the [route
finder quiz](/route-finder) will point you at the right one in a couple of
minutes, and from there the banking side of things is far more
straightforward than it looks from the outside.
