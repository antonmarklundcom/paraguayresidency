Two different things get called "the Interpol check" in conversations about Paraguay residency, and mixing them up is one of the more common sources of confusion in a document file. Separating them clearly, before you request anything, saves a step later — and for British readers, saves ordering the wrong thing from the wrong service entirely.

## The check Paraguay runs, and the certificate you provide

The first is internal to the Paraguayan process: immigration authorities screen applicants against international law-enforcement records as part of reviewing a residency application. You do not request this, arrange it, or receive a document for it — it happens on their side, using the identity information already in your file.

The second is the document you are actually asked to provide: a police or background certificate issued by the competent authority in your own country of nationality (or, in some cases, your country of recent residence). For a British applicant, that authority is ACRO Criminal Records Office, and the document is the ACRO Police Certificate, requested specifically for use overseas — not a Disclosure and Barring Service (DBS) check, which is a different product for a different purpose. This is the "police certificate" referenced throughout our nationality-specific document guides. Confusion arises because some countries' issuing process for that certificate is administered through their national Interpol bureau as the office that handles international-use requests — Interpol is the *channel* their government uses to issue the paper, not a separate certificate Paraguay is asking for on top of it.

## Why the naming varies by country

Whether your national certificate is called a "police clearance certificate," a "certificate of good conduct," a "criminal record extract," or informally "the Interpol certificate" depends entirely on which authority in your country issues it and how that country's own guidance describes it. <Fact k="documents.police_certificate_validity" site="residency" /> applies regardless of what the certificate is called locally — the recency window matters more than the label on the document.

Do not assume the presence or absence of the word "Interpol" on a certificate changes whether Paraguay accepts it. What matters is:

- **Issuer.** Confirm the certificate comes from the authority your country's own guidance names as competent for a criminal-record check "for use abroad" or "for immigration purposes" — not a private background-check company, and not a certificate produced for an unrelated purpose such as domestic employment. For the UK, that authority is ACRO, and the DBS is not a substitute.
- **Scope.** A local or regional check is not always the same as a national one. If you have lived in more than one country as an adult, more than one certificate may be required — this comes up often for applicants who moved countries mid-career.
- **Form.** The certificate needs to be the kind of official document that can carry an apostille (see our [apostille and translation guide](/guides/documents/apostilles-and-translations)), not a printout or an online verification page with no signature or seal.

## Where this fits in your filing sequence

The police certificate is one part of the wider document set — alongside your birth certificate and, where applicable, your marriage certificate — that gets apostilled and then translated for the Paraguayan immigration office.
