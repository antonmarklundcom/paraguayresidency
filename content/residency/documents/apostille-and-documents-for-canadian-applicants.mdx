Canada's paperwork changed shape recently, and a document folder assembled under the old rules can be confusing now. Since January 2024, Canada has been a member of the Hague Apostille Convention. That does not mean every certificate in your file is automatically ready. It means the route for authenticating them has changed, and the change is recent enough that not every source describing it agrees.

For Canadian applicants, the useful first move is to stop assuming last year's legalisation instructions still apply. Work out what each document is, who issued it, and which authentication route now covers it, before deciding it needs consular involvement at all.

## Canada's federal system means more than one issuer

A Canadian birth or marriage certificate is normally a provincial or territorial record, issued by the vital-statistics registry of the relevant province or territory, not by a federal office. A Canadian passport tells you nothing about where you were born or married; it only tells you which country issued the passport. Check the certificate itself for the issuing province or territory before assuming your current address identifies it.

If the underlying event happened outside Canada, treat that separately again. A foreign-issued birth or marriage certificate does not enter the Canadian apostille system just because you later became a Canadian citizen. Note the issuing country on your checklist before requesting anything, so we can flag whether a different country's authentication process applies instead.

## Since January 2024, the apostille route applies

Canada and Paraguay are both members of the Hague Apostille Convention, with Canada's accession taking effect from January 2024. For documents covered by the Convention, an apostille now generally replaces the consular legalisation chain that used to apply to Canadian records sent abroad. It authenticates the relevant signature or seal on the document; it does not decide whether Paraguay accepts that particular certificate for your application.

Because Canada's arrangements are federal, more than one office can be a competent authority depending on the document and the issuing province or territory. [Global Affairs Canada](https://www.international.gc.ca/) is a starting point for the national apostille programme, and some provinces also handle authentication for their own records. Confirm the competent authority for your exact document rather than assuming one office covers everything Canadian.

Because the change is recent, do not rely on an older description of "Canadian legalisation" you may have seen before 2024, whether from a previous applicant, a general relocation guide, or an assumption carried over from a different country's process. Check the current guidance for the specific document you hold.

## The RCMP check is its own document

For a Canadian applicant, the likely starting point for the criminal-record part of the file is a certificate based on a search through the Royal Canadian Mounted Police, generally requested through a fingerprint-based process. Confirm the accepted request route and certificate form for your circumstances; a local police records check obtained for a different purpose, such as employment or volunteering, may not be the same product Paraguay expects.

Expect an official certificate identifying you and reporting the result of that records search. Review the document's stated purpose where one appears, along with the issuing details, before treating it as ready. Ask which form of that certificate can proceed through the apostille route, and separately, whether Paraguay accepts that form for a residency file. Neither question answers the other automatically.

If a private accredited body assisted with fingerprint collection or submission, keep that role distinct from the RCMP as the issuing authority. The apostille attaches to the official record you receive, not to the convenience of the service that helped you request it.

## Canadian originals still need local Paraguayan translation

Our [apostille and translation guide](/guides/documents/apostilles-and-translations) sets the rule that applies here too: use a certified or sworn translator recognised locally in Paraguay. A Canadian document being in English, or in French with an English version attached, does not change that requirement, and a translation certified in Canada does not substitute for it.

Plan the translation around the final civil record, RCMP-based certificate, and any marriage or name-change evidence your file needs. Have the translator work from the complete package, including the apostille and any attached endorsements, so the Spanish version corresponds to what is actually being filed. Authentication normally comes before translation for that reason.

Check names before that handoff. A hyphenated surname, a name recorded differently in French and English versions of the same document, or a name changed at marriage should be flagged with supporting evidence, not smoothed over during translation.

## Leave with a checked folder

Before sending originals anywhere, keep legible reference scans and a note of who holds each document. When it returns, check that the apostille belongs to that specific document and that any attachments are intact. Review the complete set before treating delivery as the end of the task.
