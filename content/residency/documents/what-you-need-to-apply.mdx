Most delays in a Paraguay residency file are not caused by the immigration
office. They are caused by a document that was issued too early, apostilled by
the wrong authority, or translated by a translator the office does not
accept. None of those are dramatic problems on their own. Stacked together,
they add weeks to a process that should take days once you are actually in
Asunción. This is the document set we ask every client to start with, in the
order that keeps it valid.

## The core set

Every applicant brings the same three categories: a birth certificate, a
police clearance from the country (or countries) where you've lived, and
proof of means. Everything else in the file — the application form, your
passport copies, photos — is administrative and doesn't have a clock
attached. These three do.

**Birth certificate.** This has to be a certified long-form copy issued by
the civil registry, not a photocopy of the one your parents kept. If your own
copy is decades old, most registries will issue a fresh certified copy on
request, sometimes by mail. Get the fresh one. An old certificate can still
work, but it invites questions an immigration official doesn't need to ask.

**Police clearance.** This is the document that causes the most re-work,
because it's the one with the shortest shelf life and the most
country-to-country variation. Some countries issue a clearance that's valid
for six months from issue date. Others issue one valid for ninety days. A
handful require you to apply in person, which matters if you've already left
the country the clearance needs to cover. If you've lived in more than one
country in the past several years, you may need a clearance from each of
them — not just your current one.

**Proof of means.** This is not a single document, it's a category, and
which version applies to you depends on your situation. For most applicants
it's a combination of recent bank statements, a letter from your bank
confirming the account is active and in good standing, and sometimes a
pension statement or an employment letter if your income is the steadier
story. The immigration office isn't asking you to prove wealth — it's asking
you to show that you can support yourself without needing local employment.
A modest, well-documented account with a clear letter tends to move faster
than a large balance with no supporting letter at all.

## Why the order matters

<Fact k="temporary.duration" /> is how long your initial residency runs
before you move to the permanent stage, but the clock that actually catches
people out is the one on the documents themselves, not the residency term.
A police clearance collected too early can expire before your appointment
even gets scheduled. An apostille attached to an expired document doesn't
revive it — it just certifies that the (now expired) document is genuine.

The practical rule is: start with whichever document is slowest to obtain in
your country, and treat everything else as scheduled backward from it. For
most nationalities that's the police clearance. For a few, it's the
apostille itself, if your country routes apostilles through a single
national office with a multi-week backlog.

## Apostille chains, and why they're not optional

An apostille doesn't say your document is true. It says the signature and
seal on it belong to an official authority your country recognizes — which
is exactly what a foreign government (Paraguay's included) needs to accept a
document it has no way to verify on its own. Because of that narrow purpose,
an apostille has to be issued by a specific body, usually a state or
national foreign affairs office, and it has to be attached to the document
in the right sequence relative to any translation. Get that sequence
backward and the apostille doesn't transfer — you re-do it. We cover the
apostille-and-translation sequence in more detail in
[our guide to apostilles and sworn translations](/guides/documents/apostilles-and-translations),
including the countries where a document needs a second layer of
legalization before an apostille will even apply to it (this shows up with
some federal systems, where a state-level document has to be certified at
the national level first).

## Common nationality-specific snags

A few patterns come up often enough that we watch for them by default:

- **Fast-expiring clearances.** Some countries issue police clearances valid
  for only 90 days. If your appointment slips, so does your clearance.
- **Federal double-legalization.** In some federal countries, a document
  issued by a state or provincial authority needs to be authenticated at the
  national level before the apostille body will touch it — one extra step,
  one extra wait.
- **Non-Hague countries.** If your country never joined the Apostille
  Convention, there's no apostille to get — the document goes through
  consular legalization instead, which is slower and routes through an
  embassy or consulate rather than a domestic office.
- **Name mismatches.** A birth certificate with a maiden name, a marriage
  certificate that doesn't match your passport spelling exactly — these get
  flagged at the counter, not before, unless someone checks first.
