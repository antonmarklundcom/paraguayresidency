If you have a drink-driving conviction from decades ago, a caution from university or a court case that is still open, you are probably wondering whether Paraguay is closed to you before you have started. This page explains what Paraguayan law actually says about criminal records, what the migration office sees, and what to do before you spend money on documents. The short version: a record is not an automatic bar, and the worst thing you can do is hide it.

## What Paraguayan law actually says

The rule is in the migration law, Ley 6984/2022, and it is narrower than most people fear. For both temporary and permanent residency, <Fact k="residency.criminal_record_refusal" />.

Four parts of that sentence matter:

- **"May refuse."** It is a power the migration office can use, not a rule that it must. Two people with similar records can get different answers depending on the offence, how long ago it was and what else is in the file.
- **"Prosecuted or convicted."** A charge that is still open counts, not only a conviction.
- **"Under Paraguayan law."** The offence is measured by what it would carry in Paraguay, not by the sentence you actually received at home. A short sentence in the UK for an offence Paraguay treats seriously can still fall inside the rule.
- **"Intentional offence."** The clause covers intentional offences (hechos dolosos). Offences committed through negligence sit outside that particular clause, although the migration office still sees them on your certificate and can still ask about them.

Repeat offending is a ground of its own, even where each offence on its own would be minor.

Two other parts of the law are worth knowing. Someone subject to an international arrest warrant can be refused entry to Paraguay altogether. And a residency obtained with false or misleading documents can be cancelled whenever that comes to light, however long ago it was granted.

## What the migration office sees

Every applicant's file carries the same checks, record or not:

- **Your home police certificate.** For British applicants that is the ACRO Police Certificate, and it has to be <Fact k="documents.police_certificate_validity" />. Our [ACRO guide](/guides/documents/uk-police-certificate-acro-for-paraguay) covers how to request it for overseas use.
- **A Paraguayan Interpol certificate**, issued in Asunción during the process: <Fact k="fees.interpol_certificate" />. The Interpol office now also checks some records directly with the applicant's country of origin, which can add time.
- **The certificate for your cédula.** A Paraguayan police record check comes later, when your identity card is issued: <Fact k="fees.police_certificate_py" />.

Our [explainer on police certificates and Interpol checks](/guides/documents/police-certificate-and-interpol-checks) separates the home-country and Paraguayan steps in full.

## Reading your ACRO certificate

ACRO decides what appears on a police certificate for overseas use under its own disclosure rules, the step-down model. It weighs how serious the offence was, how old you were and how long ago it happened. The certificate then reads one of three ways:

- **No Trace:** nothing is held.
- **No Live Trace:** information is held but has stepped down and is not listed.
- **A list of disclosed matters**, which can include cautions, convictions and impending prosecutions.

Two points catch British applicants out. First, whether a conviction is spent under UK rehabilitation law and whether it appears on an ACRO certificate are separate questions, so don't assume one from the other. Second, a No Live Trace certificate tells a reader who knows the phrase that there is something old on file. It is a clean certificate for most purposes, but be ready to explain it in a sentence if you are asked.

ACRO publishes its current disclosure rules. Read them, but the quickest way to know what Paraguay will see is to request the certificate itself.

## Records from other countries

If you have lived outside the UK in recent years, your file needs a police certificate from that country as well as the ACRO one, and a matter recorded there shows up on that certificate, not on ACRO's. The law makes no distinction: an offence committed abroad is weighed the same way as one committed in Paraguay. Each certificate follows its own country's disclosure rules, so a matter that has dropped off one record can still appear on another. Collect every certificate you will need before you decide anything, and read them side by side.

## How different records tend to play out

This is general guidance, not a prediction for your case. The decision belongs to the migration office.

| Your situation | How the law frames it | What we suggest |
|---|---|---|
| No Trace | Nothing to weigh | A normal application |
| No Live Trace | Old information held, not disclosed | A normal application, with a short explanation ready |
| One old, minor conviction | Outside the refusal clause if the offence would not reach the law's threshold in Paraguay | Get the court record and talk to us before filing |
| A serious intentional offence (violence, drugs, fraud and similar) | Likely inside the clause, so refusal is possible | Take legal advice before paying any fees |
| Charges still open | Being prosecuted is enough under the law | Wait until the matter is resolved |
| Several convictions, even minor ones | Repeat offending is its own ground | Legal advice first |

Motoring offences are a frequent worry, and there is no single answer. It depends on the offence, how Paraguayan law classifies it and whether it appears on your certificate at all. Send us what the certificate says rather than a description of it.

## Why hiding it never works

You can't edit an official police certificate, and trying to route round it (a certificate from a different country, a document that "leaves things out", a provider who promises to make it go away) turns a manageable question into a false-document problem. As above, the law lets the migration office cancel a residency obtained with false or misleading documents at any point.

There is a longer-term reason too. If you ever want Paraguayan citizenship, good conduct is one of the constitutional conditions, and an honest residency file is the best foundation for showing it. Our [citizenship page](/residency/citizenship) explains how that stage works.

## Before you apply: a sensible order

1. **Request your ACRO certificate first**, before you order, apostille or translate anything else. Its issue date starts the recency clock, so time the request with your filing plan in mind, and tell us if that is a problem.
2. **If anything is disclosed, get the court paperwork** that shows the offence, the outcome and the date. The court that dealt with the case can usually issue a certificate or memorandum of conviction.
3. **Talk to us, or to a lawyer, before paying any Paraguayan fees.** Serious or recent matters call for a lawyer's view. Our guide to [choosing a residency agent or lawyer](/guides/documents/choosing-a-paraguay-residency-agent) explains who does what.
4. **If the answer is "not yet", ask what would change it**: an open case closing, time passing, or a different route.
