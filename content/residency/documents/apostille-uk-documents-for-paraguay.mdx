If you are British and putting together a Paraguay residency file, the apostille step runs through one office for the whole of the UK — the FCDO's Legalisation Office — but which documents need it, and what needs to happen before it, depends on the document itself. This is where we see the most avoidable delays in a UK applicant's file, more often than the police certificate.

## What actually needs an apostille

For most applicants, three kinds of UK document need to go through this route: your birth certificate, your marriage certificate (if applicable), and in some cases a degree certificate, if your residency route or a supporting element of your file relies on it. Each one is a public or quasi-public document that Paraguay's immigration office cannot verify on its own, so the apostille stands in for that verification.

**Birth certificate.** Order a certified copy from the relevant registry — the General Register Office for England and Wales, National Records of Scotland, or the General Register Office for Northern Ireland — rather than relying on an old family copy. Check which registry issued the version you hold; the UK does not have one single civil registration system, even though the apostille route that follows is national.

**Marriage certificate.** The same registration-authority logic applies. If you married outside the UK, the certificate may need to be authenticated through the issuing country's own system instead of the FCDO route — flag this with us before assuming your UK marriage certificate covers a wedding that took place abroad.

**Degree certificate.** Not every applicant needs this apostilled, but where it supports your file, a university degree certificate typically needs a preliminary certification step before the FCDO will attach an apostille to it, because it is not issued by a civil registration authority in the same way a birth certificate is. Confirm the current required route for your specific institution and document rather than assuming the same process as your birth certificate applies.

## Why some documents need an extra step first

The FCDO's [official legalisation guidance](https://www.gov.uk/get-document-legalised) distinguishes documents it can apostille directly from documents that need a prior certification — commonly by a solicitor, notary, or the issuing body itself — before the apostille can attach to them. A degree certificate is the clearest example most applicants run into; some professional certificates and photocopies work the same way.

Do not add an extra certification step out of caution if your document doesn't need one — it wastes time and money without helping your file. Equally, do not skip a required step because a different document in your folder didn't need it. Check each document against current FCDO guidance, or send it to us and we will confirm the route before you post anything.

## Translate only after the apostille is attached

Once a UK document carries its apostille, the next step is a Spanish translation — but only through a sworn translator recognised locally in Paraguay, not a certified translator based in the UK. This is the rule we hold every nationality to, and it catches British applicants as often as anyone else, because a UK-certified translation looks entirely legitimate and is still not the document Paraguay's office will accept.

Send the translator the complete package once it comes back from the FCDO: the original document, the apostille itself, and any attached endorsements. The Spanish version needs to correspond to what is actually being filed, apostille included, not just the original certificate on its own. Our [apostille and translation guide](/guides/documents/apostilles-and-translations) covers this sequencing in more depth, and it is the same sequencing that applies to your [ACRO police certificate](/guides/documents/uk-police-certificate-acro-for-paraguay).

## Names, again

Before sending anything to the FCDO or a translator, check that the name on each document matches your current passport. A birth certificate under a previous surname, a marriage certificate that changes your name, or a middle name recorded inconsistently across documents should be flagged with supporting evidence — a decree, a deed poll, a second certificate — rather than left for the translator to quietly reconcile. Translation conveys a document; it does not correct one.
