If you are comparing Paraguay residency agents, lawyers and consultants, you are probably reading several websites that all promise the same thing. This page gives you the questions that separate them. We are one of the providers you might be comparing, so read it with that in mind: every test below applies to us as well, and we answer each one at the end.

## Do you need an agent at all?

No. Paraguayan law does not require anyone to represent you, and the migration office (DNM) accepts applications filed by the applicant in person. The government fee is the same whoever files: <Fact k="fees.temporary_residency" />.

What an agent really sells is time, Spanish and fewer mistakes. A typical file means ordering documents in the UK, getting them apostilled by the FCDO, having them translated by a sworn translator in Paraguay, booking appointments in Asunción, collecting a local Interpol certificate and then following the file until it is approved. People who do it themselves usually manage. The ones who struggle tend to lose weeks on the order of steps (apostille before translation, always), on a certificate that expired while they waited, or on a request for one more document they did not understand.

Doing it yourself is a reasonable choice if:

- you read and speak Spanish well enough for a government counter,
- you can spend unhurried time in Asunción, and
- your case is ordinary: no criminal record, a clear income source and the standard temporary route.

If any of those are missing, paying someone is usually cheaper than a second trip. Our [document checklist for applicants](/guides/documents/what-you-need-to-apply) shows the full list either way.

## Agent, lawyer, notary or DIY: who does what

The titles overlap, and some providers use several at once. This is what each one normally does in a residency case.

| Option | What they do | When it fits | What to check |
|---|---|---|---|
| Doing it yourself | You collect, file and follow up in person | Simple case, good Spanish, time in Asunción | That you have the latest checklist and the fee table |
| Residency agent (gestor or gestoría) | Prepares your file, books appointments, goes with you, follows the case | Most ordinary applications | Fixed fee in writing, a named case handler, refund terms |
| Lawyer (abogado) | Everything an agent does, plus legal advice, representation and appeals | A criminal record, a refusal, an investment route, a company | That the lawyer is registered, and who actually does the work |
| Notary (escribano público) | Certifies signatures and copies, drafts powers of attorney and deeds | One-off steps, a property purchase | The registry number, and exactly what you are signing |
| Sworn translator (traductor público) | Translates your apostilled documents into Spanish | Every foreign document in English | That the translator is recognised in Paraguay |

A good agent works with a translator and, when needed, an escribano, and tells you which of them you are paying for.

## Six questions to ask before you pay anyone

### 1. Is the fee fixed, and what exactly does it cover?

Ask for a single written quote that separates three things: the provider's own fee, each government fee, and what is paid to third parties such as translators, the escribano or a courier. Government fees in Paraguay are set in minimum daily wages, <Fact k="fees.basis" />, so a quote given in May can be out of date in July. A careful provider tells you that rather than letting you find out.

"From" prices are fine as a starting point. Just make sure you know what moves the price from the "from" figure to yours: family members, extra certificates, a second trip, the cédula.

### 2. Who prepares my file, and who goes with me?

You want a name. Ask who checks your documents before anything is apostilled, who books your appointments, and who stands next to you at the migration office. Ask how many steps need you in person and which can be done with a power of attorney. If the answer is "our team handles it", ask again.

### 3. What does the escribano do in my case?

An escribano is a public notary. In a residency file, they might certify copies or signatures, or prepare a power of attorney (poder) that lets someone act for you while you are out of the country. Paraguayan notaries work under a numbered notarial registry, and a genuine one will give you their name and registry number without hesitation.

If you are asked to sign a power of attorney, read what it allows. A power limited to your residency and cédula applications is normal. A general power over your affairs, your bank accounts or property is not needed for residency, and you should not sign one.

### 4. What are the refund terms?

Ask what happens in each of these cases, and get the answer in writing:

- you change your mind before anything is filed,
- a document is rejected and has to be redone,
- the application is refused,
- government fees rise before you file.

Money already paid to the government is usually gone once paid, so the useful question is what the provider keeps of its own fee. Terms that only cover "our fault" cases are common. Terms that do not exist are a warning.

### 5. Who holds my original documents?

You will hand over originals: apostilled certificates, a birth certificate, sometimes a marriage certificate. Ask for a signed list of what you handed over and when you get it back. Your passport is a different matter. It should not be kept overnight by anyone, and a provider that asks to hold it is doing something unusual.

### 6. Will you tell me if this route is wrong for me?

A provider who files every case the same way is not advising you. Ask whether temporary residency is the right route for you, whether you might qualify for something faster or simpler, and what would make them tell you to wait. If you have a criminal record, a gap in your documents or unusual income, the right provider talks about it before taking your money. We explain how records are weighed in [our guide to residency with a criminal record](/guides/documents/paraguay-residency-with-a-criminal-record).

## Red flags

Any one of these is a reason to slow down. Two is a reason to walk away.

- **Guaranteed approval or a guaranteed date.** Nobody controls the migration office's decisions or its queue.
- **Offers to skip a step.** The police certificate is part of every file, and it has to be <Fact k="documents.police_certificate_validity" />. The Interpol check in Asunción is part of every file too. Anyone offering a way round either is offering something that gets residencies cancelled.
- **Extra "fees" for official certificates.** The Interpol certificate in Asunción has an official fee, <Fact k="fees.interpol_certificate" />. Pay that amount against an official receipt, and nothing on the side.
- **Out-of-date rules.** If a provider still asks you for the old fixed bank deposit, their information is stale: the deposit is <Fact k="solvency.deposit_abolished" />. Current proof of means is <Fact k="solvency.requirement" />.
- **Cash only, no contract, no receipts.**
- **Pressure to buy property or invest through them** so you "qualify". Investment residency has its own route with its own rules; the team at [paraguayinvestorpass.com](https://paraguayinvestorpass.com) covers it separately.
- **Claims of special contacts inside the migration office.** Files are approved on documents, and a provider who sells access is selling risk.
