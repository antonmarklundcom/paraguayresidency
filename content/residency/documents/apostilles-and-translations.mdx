Two small steps cause a disproportionate share of the delays we see: getting
a document apostilled, and getting it translated. Neither step is
complicated on its own. The trouble comes from doing them out of order, or
from assuming any qualified translator will do. Here's what each step
actually accomplishes, and how to avoid re-doing either one — with the UK
route named specifically where it differs from other countries.

## What an apostille actually certifies

This is the part people get wrong most often: an apostille does not certify
that your document is true. It certifies that the signature, seal or stamp
on the document belongs to an official who is authorised to issue it. That's
it. A birth certificate with an apostille attached is not being vouched for
by a foreign government — it's being confirmed as genuinely issued by the
registrar whose name is on it.

That narrow purpose is exactly why it works across borders. Paraguay's
immigration office has no way to call your country's civil registry and
confirm a signature. An apostille replaces that phone call with a
standardised certificate that any country in the Hague Apostille Convention
agrees to recognise. It's a shortcut for trust between governments, not a
statement about the content of your document.

## The Hague Apostille Convention, briefly

The convention is a treaty that lets member countries skip the older,
slower process — consular legalisation — in favour of a single certificate
issued by a designated authority inside the document's own country. Both
the UK and Paraguay are members, so a UK document apostilled through the
FCDO's Legalisation Office is accepted in Paraguay without any further
authentication by a Paraguayan consulate. The UK route sits alongside a
national arrangement rather than a state-by-state one, which simplifies the
"which office" question a UK applicant would otherwise have to ask.

Not every country is a member. If yours isn't, there's no apostille to
obtain — your document goes through consular legalisation instead, which
usually means a visit to (or a mailed submission through) Paraguay's embassy
or consulate in your country. It takes longer, and it's one more reason to
start this step as early as possible rather than assuming it will be quick.

One more wrinkle for UK applicants specifically: a document issued by a
professional body, a solicitor's certified copy, or a document that isn't a
plain public-authority original may need an extra certification step before
the FCDO will apply an apostille to it. This adds a step, not a dead end,
but it adds time you should plan for rather than discover the week before
your appointment. Our [UK apostille guide](/guides/documents/apostille-uk-documents-for-paraguay)
goes through this in more detail.

## Why the translator matters — not just the translation

Paraguay's immigration office doesn't accept "a certified translation" as a
category. It accepts translations from specific translators it recognises,
often called sworn or public translators (traductores públicos), who are
registered to produce translations with legal standing in Paraguay. A
translation done by a perfectly competent, certified translator in the UK —
the kind that satisfies a university or a visa office elsewhere — can still
be rejected here, simply because that translator isn't on the list
Paraguay's office actually checks against.

This is the single most avoidable rejection we see, and also the hardest
one to catch yourself, because nothing about a translation done abroad
looks wrong until it's already in front of an official who doesn't
recognise the translator's registration. The safer path is to have
translation done locally, by someone whose registration the office already
knows, once the original document has cleared its own legalisation step.

## The sequencing problem

Apostille first, translate second. Almost always. The apostille attaches to
the original document — the birth certificate, the clearance, whatever it
is — certifying the issuing signature. If you translate the document before
it's apostilled, you've produced a translation of a document that, from
Paraguay's side, hasn't yet been certified as genuine. Depending on the
office and the document, that can mean the translation gets redone once the
apostille is in hand, or the apostille itself needs to reference the
translation in a way your original apostille didn't account for.

Getting this backward is not fatal, but it is expensive in the way most
process mistakes are: not in money so much as in the two or three weeks it
takes to notice the problem, re-send the original for its apostille, and
then get it translated again by someone who is actually registered where it
counts. Cédula timing: <Fact k="cedula.timeline" />, and every week spent re-doing a
translation is a week that timeline pushes back.
