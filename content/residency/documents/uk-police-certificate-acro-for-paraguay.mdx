British applicants ask us the same question in slightly different words every week: "which police certificate do I actually need?" The honest, short answer is the ACRO Police Certificate, requested specifically for overseas use — and it is worth separating that from a second, unrelated fee that comes up later in Asunción, which some UK-based guidance conflates with it.

## The document: ACRO, not DBS

ACRO Criminal Records Office is the UK body that issues the police certificate accepted for immigration and residency applications abroad. It is a different product from a Disclosure and Barring Service (DBS) check, which is built for UK employment, volunteering and safeguarding purposes and is not the document Paraguay's process is asking for. If you already hold a DBS certificate from a previous job, it does not substitute for the ACRO certificate — the issuing authority, the purpose stated on the document, and the intended recipient are all different.

When you apply, request the certificate specifically for the purpose of overseas residency or immigration use, not a general or employment-purpose certificate. ACRO's own guidance sets out the current application routes, the information you will need to provide, and its published fee and turnaround time for the overseas service — confirm those directly with ACRO, since both are reviewed periodically and we would rather point you to the source than quote a figure that goes stale.

## The order of steps

The sequence that avoids a costly re-do is fixed, and it is the same one we hold every nationality to:

1. **Apply to ACRO** for the Police Certificate, specifying overseas/immigration use as the purpose.
2. **Receive the certificate** and check the personal details, the stated purpose and the issuing signature before moving on.
3. **Send it for an apostille through the FCDO's Legalisation Office.** This is the UK's single national apostille route — there is no separate regional office to use instead. The apostille authenticates the ACRO signature; it does not vouch for the content of the certificate itself.
4. **Only then, arrange the Spanish translation** — through a sworn translator recognised locally in Paraguay, not a certified translator in the UK. Translating before the apostille is attached is the single most common re-do we see across every nationality, not just British applicants; our [apostille and translation guide](/guides/documents/apostilles-and-translations) explains why the order matters.

Getting this backward does not ruin your file, but it does cost weeks: the translation has to be redone once the apostille is properly attached, because the translator needs the complete, authenticated document in front of them, not the certificate alone.

## The Interpol certificate in Asunción — a separate, later step

Here is the point that causes the most confusion, and it is worth stating plainly: once you are in Paraguay, part of the residency and cédula process involves a local certificate issued by the Interpol department of the Policía Nacional in Asunción. This is a Paraguayan document, obtained locally, with its own current fee: <Fact k="fees.interpol_certificate" />. It is not something you arrange from the UK, and it is not the same thing as your ACRO certificate.

Some UK-facing guidance blurs these two into one "Interpol check," which leads people to either pay a UK-based intermediary for something unnecessary or assume their ACRO certificate already covers the local Paraguayan step. It doesn't. Our [broader explainer on police certificates and Interpol checks](/guides/documents/police-certificate-and-interpol-checks) separates the two in full, including the internal screening Paraguayan immigration runs on every applicant's file as a matter of course — that screening is not a document you request at all.
