Before Brexit, a Briton who fancied Portugal simply moved there. Now a British citizen needs a residence permit for Portugal just as for Paraguay, and the two end up on many of the same shortlists. They suit different people. This page compares them on the things that decide most British moves: getting the permit, tax, the State Pension, healthcare, cost and the long road to a passport. We are a Paraguay residency team, so weigh our view of Portugal accordingly; where Portugal is better, we say so.

We don't quote Portuguese thresholds on this page. They change with the Portuguese minimum wage and the state budget, so check them with the Portuguese consulate before you rely on any figure. The Paraguayan figures below come from our facts register, with sources.

## Getting the permit

**Portugal** now works like most visa countries for Britons. You apply at a Portuguese consulate for a national visa, most often the D7 (passive income, the retiree's route) or the D8 (remote work), enter Portugal, and then apply for a residence permit with the immigration agency, AIMA. Appointment delays at AIMA have been widely reported, so allow time. Portugal's golden visa still exists but no longer accepts property purchases.

**Paraguay** runs on documents. You collect a birth certificate, an ACRO police certificate and proof of means, apostilled by the FCDO, have them translated in Paraguay and file in Asunción. Temporary residency lasts <Fact k="temporary.duration" />. There is no consular visa step for British citizens: the FCDO confirms you can enter as a visitor and apply from there. Our [guide for British applicants](/guides/documents/apostille-and-documents-for-british-applicants) lists the documents.

Both countries attach presence conditions. In Paraguay the rule on temporary residency is <Fact k="temporary.presence_rule" />. Portugal expects residents to actually live there, and absences affect renewals; check the current limits with AIMA.

## Tax: opposite starting points

This is where the two differ most, and where most British movers make up their minds.

**Portugal** taxes its residents on their worldwide income, as the UK does. The non-habitual resident (NHR) regime, which let many British retirees pay little Portuguese tax on UK pensions, closed to new applicants from 2024. Its replacement, known as IFICI, is aimed at researchers, tech and other specific professions, and does not help most retirees or passive investors.

**Paraguay** taxes on a territorial basis: <Fact k="tax.territorial_rate" />, and <Fact k="tax.foreign_income_treatment" />. For someone living on foreign income, that is the draw.

The UK side changes the picture. The UK and Portugal have a double taxation treaty. The UK and Paraguay don't: <Fact k="uk.paraguay_tax_treaty" />. So a UK pension or UK rental income can stay taxable in the UK, whichever country you pick, and in Paraguay's case there is no treaty to move the taxing right. Whether you have left UK tax residence at all is decided by HMRC's Statutory Residence Test, which our [UK tax guide](/guides/taxes/uk-tax-when-moving-to-paraguay) explains. Compare your own figures with a UK tax adviser before tax decides the country.

## The State Pension and healthcare: Portugal's clear advantage

For British pensioners, Portugal wins two points outright.

First, the State Pension. Portugal is in the EEA, so the UK pays the annual increase there. In Paraguay your UK State Pension is <Fact k="uk.state_pension_paraguay" />. Over a long retirement that difference is large. Our guide to [retiring in Paraguay on a UK State Pension](/guides/living-in-paraguay/uk-state-pension-in-paraguay) works through it.

Second, healthcare. UK State Pensioners living in Portugal can usually register an S1, which gives them access to the Portuguese state health service at the UK's expense. There is no S1 for Paraguay and no NHS equivalent for foreign residents, so you budget for private cover. Private care in Asunción is well regarded and cheaper than in Western Europe, but it is a cost you carry yourself.

Paraguay is markedly cheaper. In Asunción the usual crowd-sourced benchmark for everyday spending is <Fact k="costofliving.overview" />. Portugal is still cheaper than much of the UK, but rents in Lisbon, Porto and the Algarve have risen fast, and many Britons who priced a Portuguese move a few years ago find it costs more than they expected. ## A second passport

**Portugal** lengthened the residence requirement for naturalisation in its 2026 nationality reform, with shorter periods kept for some groups. Check the current rule when you plan; it has changed more than once.

**Paraguay** asks for <Fact k="citizenship.years" />, then an application to the Supreme Court. Paraguay only admits dual nationality through treaties, so check whether you can keep your British passport before you set your heart on it. Our [Paraguay citizenship page](/residency/citizenship) explains the stages.

## Lifestyle

Portugal is Europe: short flights home, an easy time zone, a large British community, a mild Atlantic climate and EU consumer protections. Paraguay is further, hotter and quieter. Asunción is a relaxed, green capital with a small but growing foreign community. Daily life runs in Spanish, and Guaraní is widely spoken too. Many British movers find that Paraguay's lower profile is part of the appeal; others miss being a short flight from family.

## Who tends to choose which

**Portugal suits** British pensioners who want the State Pension to keep rising and state healthcare through the S1, people who want to stay close to family in the UK, and anyone who values EU life over cost.

**Paraguay suits** people living on foreign income who want a territorial tax system, people for whom cost of living is a deciding factor, and those who want a straightforward, document-driven residency, including as a second residency held alongside life elsewhere.

Some people end up with both: a life in Europe and a Paraguayan residency kept as a plan B. That works if your travel pattern meets both countries' presence conditions.
